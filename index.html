I’m Anirudha Karande, a graduate researcher in robot learning at WPI.

I am an M.S. student in Robotics Engineering at Worcester Polytechnic Institute, advised by Prof. Jing Xiao.

My research goal is to build robots that learn physical skills the way people do: from a few demonstrations, through safe practice, and over a lifetime. I focus on the part of a skill you can’t see, how firmly to hold when touching something, and learn it by combining reinforcement learning with optimization and physics. Towards this goal, I work on turning a few human demonstrations into physically verified training data, on safe exploration in contact, and on continual learning, where a robot gains new skills without losing old ones. I draw on computational sensorimotor learning, taking cues from how people adapt their own bodies. I believe such robots will extend what skilled people can do, not replace them.

I am applying to PhD programs in robot learning.

Now

When should a robot be stiff, and when should it be soft?

When a robot arm touches something, it behaves a bit like a spring. A stiff spring follows its plan closely but pushes hard when the plan is wrong. A soft one gives way, but drifts. A robot can learn to change this stiffness as it moves, much as you tense or relax your arm.

Many studies report that learning to change stiffness beats keeping it fixed. I want to know when that is really true, and how to keep a robot safe while it is still learning. I would rather report an honest no than a lucky yes.

I also read a lot about how the brain controls movement. People adjust the stiffness of their arms all the time, and how they do it is the best guide I have found. The notes below are what I have learned.
