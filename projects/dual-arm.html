How Hard to Hold It: Two Robot Arms and Seven Everyday Objects

[Project]

A child finds out how heavy something is by lifting it; these arms have to do the same. Two Franka Panda arms carry everyday objects whose weight, contents, friction and fragility are hidden. Every 20 ms the robot sets how hard to grip, how stiffly to hold the object, how stiffly to keep its hands' spacing, and how much weight to carry by feedforward. A policy trained with reinforcement learning carries heavy boxes of books that the best fixed setting drops (85% vs 45%), by gripping with the load it feels. The stiffness turned out not to matter here; the grip did.

Setup

Seven objects: a laptop, a folding chair, a basket of apples, a pot of water, a cutting board, a box of books and a pizza box. Each episode the arms grasp, lift, carry the object sideways and back through an unannounced bump, and set it down. Mass, contents, friction, fragility and the object's exact position change every time and are never observed; the robot knows only what kind of object it holds.

Optimisation: grasps and arm poses

Grasps are chosen by minimising a cost \(J\) (centre of mass under the line between the hands, comfortable thickness, deep pad) over pinches found on the objects' collision hulls, subject to robustness: closing the real hand model with the object moved anywhere on the 2σ boundary of the perception error, each finger must meet a flat face,

$$p^\star = \arg\min_p J(p) \quad \text{s.t.}\quad \angle\big(n(p, \delta), c\big) \le 25^\circ \ \ \forall\, \delta \in \partial\mathcal{E}_{2\sigma}.$$

Arm poses come from differential inverse kinematics, one small quadratic program per step:

$$\min_{\Delta q}\ \textstyle\sum_{k} \lVert J_k \Delta q - e_k \rVert_W^2 + \lambda \lVert \Delta q \rVert^2 \quad \text{s.t.}\quad q_{\min} \le q + \Delta q \le q_{\max}.$$

Variable impedance control

The hands' tracking errors split into a common mode \(e_c = \tfrac12(e_L + e_R)\), which moves the object, and a differential mode \(e_d = \tfrac12(e_L - e_R)\) along the line \(u\) between the hands, which sets how they press on it:

$$F_{L,R} = \underbrace{K_o\, e_c + D_o \dot e_c}_{\text{object}} \pm \underbrace{\big(K_{sq} u u^\top + K_\perp (I - u u^\top)\big) e_d}_{\text{squeeze}} + \tfrac12 f_{ff}\, \hat z,$$

with \(D = 2\zeta\sqrt{K m}\) and \(\tau = J^\top F\) plus a null-space posture term. An energy tank caps the energy that stiffness changes inject, \(\tfrac12 \Delta K \lVert e \rVert^2 \le T\). It runs at 500 Hz in C.

Learning

The policy sees six steps of what the robot measures (hand errors and velocities, jaw openings, wrist forces, motion phase, object category) and outputs the four knobs on log scales. It is trained with PPO,

$$\max_\theta\ \mathbb{E}\Big[\min\big(\rho_t \hat A_t,\ \operatorname{clip}(\rho_t, 1 \pm \epsilon)\,\hat A_t\big)\Big], \qquad r_t = \text{outcome} - \alpha f_t - \beta \log K_t,$$

rewarding a clean carry and charging a little for grip and stiffness. The critic also sees the hidden properties; the policy never does. Three seeds, 20 million steps each, on a CPU cluster.

Does the stiffness matter?

Stiffness fights position errors from forces you cannot predict; holding is limited by the force through the fingers, which only grip changes. Adapting stiffness should pay where both sides cost something: tight placement of unknown heavy loads with a delayed control loop, boxes carried between two palms, top-heavy objects and impacts. That is the next step.
