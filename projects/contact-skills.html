Learning Contact-Rich Skills from a Few Demonstrations

Anirudha Karande and Jing Xiao

[Paper in preparation · first author, led independently]

Motivation

Robots are starting to work in factories, but the hardest moments of their jobs are moments of contact: fitting a part into a fixture, seating a tool, pressing along a surface. Success depends on how the robot moves and how firmly it holds at the instant it touches, and that is exactly what is hardest to teach. A person can show a robot where to go, but not how stiff to be, and collecting many demonstrations is slow and expensive.

People do this without thinking. When we put a key in a lock or a plug in a socket, we move to find the opening and, at the same time, change how firmly we hold: loose enough to let the part slide along the edges into place, firm enough to push it home. Motion and stiffness work together, and both change from moment to moment. We want robots to learn both, the way people use them.

The internet taught machines to talk, but nothing written down teaches them to touch: nobody has ever recorded how hard to press. Each source of robot data covers only part of it. Video shows the motion but never the force, teleoperation records forces but only at human speed, and simulation is fast but least accurate exactly where contact happens.

Our goal is to learn contact-rich skills from just a few human demonstrations. We turn those few examples into a large amount of practice in simulation, check that what the robot learns actually works, and bring the skill back to the real robot. The skill still comes from people; the robot multiplies it rather than replacing it.

What our method delivers

  • A few demonstrations, at scale. A handful of human demonstrations becomes a large set of training examples, produced by computation rather than hours of teleoperation.
  • Grounded in physics. Every example is checked in physics simulation, with contact, force and friction, before the robot learns from it, so it learns from what actually works, not from what only looks right.
  • Motion and stiffness together. Like a person, the robot learns both where to move and how firmly to hold at each moment of contact.
  • High-precision assembly. Parts go into holes with clearances as low as 0.1 mm per side.
  • Generalizes across geometries and tolerances. One policy handles varied peg shapes and clearances, including shapes it never saw in training.
  • High success rate. On the hardest insertions it succeeds about twice as often as the best fixed-stiffness controller.
  • Zero-shot across robots. A policy learned on a Franka Panda runs on a KUKA iiwa with no retraining.
  • Gentle, readable contact. The robot commands an explicit stiffness and keeps contact forces low.
  • Real to sim to real. It starts from real demonstrations and is built to bring the skill back to the real robot.

Precision, generalization, success-rate and cross-robot results are from simulation.
