Safe Variable Impedance Reinforcement Learning on the Constraint Manifold

Anirudha Karande and Jing Xiao

[Paper]

TL;DR

Problem. In variable-impedance RL the policy commands joint motion and stiffness, so the normal contact force \(F_n \approx K_n(\ell)\,\delta(q)\) depends on both: a force limit can be crossed by stiffening alone. Safe-RL filters (constraint-manifold projection, SafeLayer) assume fixed controller gains and constrain motion only; their constraint Jacobian has no \(\partial g/\partial \ell\) column, so this direction is invisible to them.

Insight. The force limit \(g_f(q, \ell) \le 0\) is a joint constraint on motion and stiffness. Lift the stiffness parameters into the controllable state, \(z = (q, \mu_j, \mu_f, \ell) \in \mathbb{R}^{28}\), and the constraint Jacobian gains the column \(\gamma\,\partial g_f/\partial \ell\) that makes stiffness safety-relevant.

Approach. Parameterise \(K = \exp(S(\ell)) \in \mathrm{SPD}(3)\), so every command is a valid stiffness and updates follow geodesics. Turn the 14 joint limits and the force limit into equalities with slack variables, \(\mathcal{M} = \{z : c(z) = 0\}\). At every control step, project the policy's command onto \(T_z\mathcal{M} = \ker J_c\) and correct drift: \(\dot z_{\text{safe}} = N_c N_c^\top \dot z_{\text{des}} - J_c^{\dagger} \Lambda\, \alpha(c)\).

Result. Franka Panda polishing in simulation, 15 N limit, 1M training steps, 3 seeds: 582 ± 269 force-drift violations, against 5,035 ± 2,620 (SafeLayer + Loewner), 5,531 ± 2,064 (SafeLayer, kinematic) and 9,691 ± 1,540 (Lagrangian): 8.6× to 16.6× fewer. In the comparison episode the peak force is 13.4 N with no violations, against 30.8 N and 21% of the time over the limit for the Lagrangian policy, with the highest reward and the tightest in-plane tracking.

Next. We take this to the real robot. Learning on the robot lets it keep improving after it leaves the lab, and because safety lives in the filter rather than in the policy's weights, it can keep learning without unlearning the force limit.

The problem

Exploring is how robots learn, and contact is where exploring hurts. A robot that learns to polish or wipe by reinforcement learning has to try things, and in contact one bad try can damage the tool, the part or the robot within a single control step. Safety filters usually guard motion: they keep the robot from moving somewhere unsafe. That is enough when its stiffness is fixed, because how hard the tool presses then depends only on where it is.

Once the robot also learns its stiffness, this breaks. The same pose is gentle when the arm is soft and dangerous when it is stiff, so a policy can press too hard just by stiffening, and a filter that watches only motion never notices.

The method

We extend constraint-manifold safe RL, which assumes a fixed controller, so that stiffness is part of the state the filter keeps safe. The filter sits between the policy and a variable impedance controller and acts on every command, in three steps.

  1. Augment the state. The stiffness parameters \(\ell\) join the joint positions \(q\) and the slack variables that turn the 14 joint limits and the force limit into equalities, in one controllable state \(z = (q, \mu_j, \mu_f, \ell) \in \mathbb{R}^{7+14+1+6} = \mathbb{R}^{28}\). The stiffness matrix is \(K = \exp(S(\ell))\), the matrix exponential of a symmetric matrix built from \(\ell\), so every stiffness the policy can ask for is symmetric positive definite, and changing \(\ell\) moves \(K\) along a geodesic of those matrices rather than a straight line.
  2. Build one manifold. On a surface with normal \(n\), the contact force is about the normal stiffness times the penetration depth, \(F_n \approx K_n(\ell)\,\delta(q)\) with \(K_n(\ell) = n^\top \exp(S(\ell))\,n\). So the force limit \(g_f(q, \ell) \le 0\) depends on stiffness as well as pose. Together with the joint limits \(g_j(q) \le 0\), it defines one constraint manifold over motion and stiffness, \(\mathcal{M} = \{z : c(z) = 0\}\).
  3. Project each command. The safe directions are the tangent space \(T_z\mathcal{M} = \ker J_c(z)\), where $$J_c = \begin{bmatrix} J_g^{\,j} & \operatorname{diag}(\mu_j) & 0 & 0 \\ \gamma\,\partial g_f/\partial q & 0 & \gamma\,\mu_f & \gamma\,\partial g_f/\partial \ell \end{bmatrix}.$$ Its last column, \(\gamma\,\partial g_f/\partial \ell\), is what a motion-only filter lacks: it makes stiffness count for safety. The filter keeps the tangent part of the policy's command and removes the rest, $$\dot z_{\text{safe}} = N_c N_c^\top \dot z_{\text{des}} - J_c^{\dagger}\,\Lambda\,\alpha(c),$$ where the columns of \(N_c\) span the tangent space and the second term pulls the state back onto \(\mathcal{M}\) if it drifts off.

The command that runs stays on the manifold, so safety comes from the filter's construction rather than from a penalty the policy has to learn, and the policy is free to explore in every direction that does not push the contact force past the limit.

Results

We trained a simulated Franka Panda to polish curved surfaces under a 15 N force limit and compared the filter with three baselines: a Lagrangian method, which adds the limit to the training objective with a learned multiplier, and two versions of SafeLayer, a filter that corrects each action before it runs. Each method was trained for one million steps with three seeds; the table counts force-limit violations over the whole run.

What matters

  • Stiffness has to enter the constraint Jacobian. Violations can be driven through \(\partial F_n/\partial \ell = \delta(q)\,\partial K_n/\partial \ell\), a direction a motion-only filter cannot see. With the \(\gamma\,\partial g_f/\partial \ell\) column in \(J_c\), the projection removes it: the kinematic SafeLayer had 5,531 ± 2,064 force-drift violations over training, ours 582 ± 269.
  • Penalties enforce the limit only in expectation. The Lagrangian method learns its multiplier from violations: 9,691 ± 1,540 over training and, in the comparison episode, a 30.8 N peak, twice the 15 N limit, above it 21% of the time.
  • SPD by construction. With \(K = \exp(S(\ell))\) the filter never has to clip or repair an indefinite matrix, and a step in \(\ell\) moves \(K\) along \(\gamma(t) = \exp\big((1-t)S_A + tS_B\big)\) rather than the straight chord between the two matrices.
  • No reward cost, and it transfers. The highest reward and the tightest in-plane tracking of all methods; lower drift than every baseline with force-sensor noise and on unseen NURBS surfaces; zero-shot on five unseen surfaces (flat, dome, three NURBS).

Takeaway

In variable-impedance RL the safe set is defined over \((q, \ell)\), not \(q\): a force limit \(K_n(\ell)\,\delta(q) \le F_{\text{con}}\) is invisible to any filter whose constraint Jacobian lacks \(\partial g_f/\partial \ell\). Lifting SPD stiffness into the controllable state and projecting every command onto the tangent space of the joint constraint manifold makes the force limit a per-step constraint, with 8.6× to 16.6× fewer violations and no loss of reward.

Next: learning on the real robot

Everything above happens in simulation. The next step is to let the robot learn on hardware, with the filter guarding every command while it practises. We think this is necessary, not just a final check, for four reasons.

Safe exploration only matters on hardware. In simulation, crossing the force limit costs a reset. On a real arm it can damage the tool, the part or the robot, and every overload adds wear. The point of keeping the constraints satisfied throughout learning, while the policy is still trying things it has never tried, is to make learning on the real robot possible. A filter that only ever runs in a simulator protects the simulator.

The guarantee is only as good as the model. The filter keeps the robot on the constraint manifold of the model it is given. Ours writes the force limit through a contact model, force as normal stiffness times penetration depth, with the surface's position and normal taken from the simulator, and assumes the controller tracks as modelled. On a real robot the surface is never exactly where the model puts it, tracking is imperfect and the force sensor is noisy, so model errors and disturbances can still push the robot over the limit. Those errors can only be measured on the robot, so that is where the filter has to be tested and its model corrected.

Simulation is weakest exactly at contact. Most robot RL stays in simulation because unsafe exploration there is free, and then has to cross the gap to the real robot. For a contact skill the gap sits where the skill lives. Friction, surface compliance and the force at first touch are hard to simulate accurately, so a policy that learned its stiffness in simulation has learned the simulator's contact. Learning on the robot trains it on the contact it will actually meet.

Robots should keep improving after they leave the lab. In the end we want a robot to get better through its own practice, on parts, tools and surfaces no one modelled. A robot allowed to practise on real parts needs a guarantee on every command, not a penalty that teaches it the limit by crossing it. In our simulation, the Lagrangian baseline crossed the force limit about 10,000 times per training run; on a real arm, each of those would be a real overload.

What changes on the robot

  • Learn the constraint as well as the policy. The filter needs to know where the surface is and which way it faces. On hardware we will learn this from the robot's own contact data, as an implicit surface whose value gives the penetration depth and whose gradient gives the normal, so that it plugs straight into the constraint Jacobian.
  • Correct at the control rate. Between two corrections the robot can drift off the manifold, and the longer the gap, the further it drifts. So the filter's correction belongs in the robot's 1 kHz torque loop, with the policy running slower above it.
  • Spend real samples carefully. Hardware time is scarce, and our simulated runs used a million steps each. Two things reduce how much data the robot needs. The filter keeps exploration on the constraint manifold, a smaller space than the full command space, so the policy does not spend samples on commands that would be cut anyway. And the task has a symmetry worth building in: \(F_n \approx K_n(\ell)\,\delta(q)\) depends on stiffness only through \(K_n = n^\top K n\), so turning the stiffness about the surface normal leaves the predicted force unchanged. A force model learned from the robot's data that respects this has less to learn, and needs fewer samples.

The aim is a robot that respects the force limit while it practises on real surfaces, and gets better at the task the more it practises.
