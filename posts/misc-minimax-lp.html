Minimax by Linear Programming

[Misc]

A robot searching for a hole it cannot see is playing a game against the hole's position. This note derives the linear program that solves any finite zero-sum game, shows the geometry behind it, and applies it to a real search problem: 25 search patterns against 49 hole positions. Every single pattern can be beaten; the right mixture of 11 cannot, and double oracle finds it while evaluating 29.5% of the game.

1. The game

A peg must find a hole whose position is uncertain by up to 8 mm. The robot picks a search pattern: spirals, rings and lawnmower sweeps at several scales and phases, or a single plunge. Each pattern visits up to 24 probe points, and the peg drops in if a probe lands within 1.5 mm of the hole. The payoff is 0 if the hole is never found and \(1 - 0.4\,k/(n-1)\) if it is found on probe \(k\) of \(n\), so finding it early is worth more. Figure 1 shows why no single pattern is safe: each one has gaps between its probes, and an adversary who knows the pattern puts the hole in a gap.

2. Mixed strategies and the linear program

Write the payoffs as a matrix \(A \in \mathbb R^{m\times n}\): rows are the robot's patterns, columns the hole positions. If the robot randomises with probabilities \(x \in \Delta_m\) and the adversary picks column \(j\), the expected payoff is \((A^\top x)_j\). Against a robot that randomises, the adversary's best reply is the worst column, so the robot's guarantee is \(\min_j (A^\top x)_j\). Maximising it is a linear program once the minimum is replaced by a variable bounded by every column:

$$\max_{x,\,v}\; v \quad\text{s.t.}\quad A^\top x \ge v\,\mathbf 1,\quad \mathbf 1^\top x = 1,\quad x \ge 0 .$$

Its dual is the adversary's problem: choose \(y \in \Delta_n\) to minimise the robot's best payoff,

$$\min_{y,\,w}\; w \quad\text{s.t.}\quad A\,y \le w\,\mathbf 1,\quad \mathbf 1^\top y = 1,\quad y \ge 0 .$$

Both programs are feasible and bounded, so strong duality gives equal optima. That equality is von Neumann's minimax theorem,

$$\max_{x\in\Delta_m}\min_{y\in\Delta_n} x^\top A y \;=\; \min_{y\in\Delta_n}\max_{x\in\Delta_m} x^\top A y \;=\; v^\star ,$$

obtained as a by-product of LP duality. One LP solve returns the value and an optimal strategy for each side.

3. The geometry: a lower envelope

With two rows the picture is exact. A mixture is one number \(p\), and each column contributes a line \(p\,A_{1j} + (1-p)\,A_{2j}\). The guarantee is the lower envelope of all the lines, and the optimal mixture sits at its peak (Figure 2). For the best pair of patterns in this game, ring:7.0mm@0deg and spiral:2.0mm@120deg, each alone guarantees 0, but mixing them with \(p = 0.53\) guarantees 0.325.

4. The full game

With all 25 patterns the LP gives a game value of 0.378. The best pure pattern guarantees 0.000, and so does the pattern with the best average payoff under a uniformly placed hole (spiral:3.0mm@60deg, average 0.557): tuning for the average case picks a strategy worth nothing against a worst case. The optimal mixture uses 11 patterns (Figure 3), and the adversary's optimal mixture concentrates on the holes that fall between probes of the heavily used patterns.

5. Exploitability

For any robot strategy \(x\), exploitability measures how much a best-responding adversary takes away from the value:

$$\operatorname{expl}(x) = v^\star - \min_j (A^\top x)_j \;\ge\; 0 ,$$

zero exactly at an optimal strategy. Here the best single pattern has exploitability 0.378, the uniform mixture over all 25 patterns 0.280, and the equilibrium mixture 0.000. Naive randomisation recovers only a little of the gap: the mixture has to be the right one.

6. Double oracle: solving without the whole matrix

On a robot, each matrix entry costs a trial, so writing \(A\) down is the expensive part. Double oracle (McMahan, Gordon and Blum, ICML 2003) avoids it. Start from one strategy per player, solve the small game, and ask each player for a best reply to the other's current mixture over its full strategy set. Add the replies and repeat. If neither best reply is new, the small game's equilibrium is an equilibrium of the full game: no strategy outside the small game does better against it, for either player. The gap between the two best replies bounds the distance to the value and shrinks to zero (Figure 4). Here the algorithm stops after evaluating 29.5% of the matrix.

7. Testing the solver

A wrong LP still returns a probability vector, so errors are silent. The checks that catch them are games with known answers: matching pennies (value 0, uniform play), rock–paper–scissors (value 0, uniform over three) and a game with a saddle point (a pure equilibrium the LP must find). The solver in this project is tested on all three before it touches the insertion game.

8. What to take away

  • Against uncertainty that is adversarial, or merely unmodelled, a deterministic plan has a worst case, and the worst case can be zero even when the average looks good.
  • The minimax strategy comes from one linear program, and its dual is the worst-case disturbance, which is worth reading as a test set.
  • When each evaluation costs a physical trial, double oracle turns "solve the game" into "find a handful of best replies".

The wider literature is in Adversaries and Games for Robot Robustness.
