Models and Policies: Forward, Inverse, Neural ODE and State-Space

[Fundamentals]

Fundamentals: optimisation · neural networks · models and policies · floating point · memory and the 1 kHz loop · GPUs · processes and the kernel · dynamics algorithms · deploying policies · policy files · how neurons compute · bits, delays and control. Measured on my workstation (Intel i7-9700, RTX 2070 SUPER, Ubuntu 24.04, Linux 6.8); code in the site's tools/posts/.

A model predicts what the world will do; a policy decides what to do. Building a model is a set of choices: predict forward or backward, step in discrete or continuous time, treat the state as observed or as hidden. I ran one small experiment for each choice. Predicting the change instead of the next state made a learned pendulum model 8 times more accurate, and an ensemble's disagreement tracked its own error over 5 s rollouts. An inverse model of a block pressed against a wall answered −2.47 N for every push, because positions and velocities do not record how hard you push; a force reading fixed it. Imitation-learned policies fit the same frame, and compound errors the way a forward model does: steering around a circle, behaviour cloning from five demonstrations ended 0.16 m off the path, while DAgger, with the same number of expert labels, matched the expert's 0.007 m. A neural ODE's one-step error stayed at or below 0.11% for time steps up to four times longer than any it was trained on, where a discrete model was off by 81%. A Kalman filter estimated velocity with half the error of a tuned low-pass filter. A selective state-space layer, the idea behind Mamba, held a marked value that no fixed-step layer could.

1. A model predicts; a policy decides

In robot learning and control, the two words answer different questions.

Model (dynamics model)Policy
AnswersIf I do \(a\) in state \(s\), what happens?In state \(s\), what should I do?
Form\(s_{t+1} = f(s_t, a_t)\), or a distribution \(p(s_{t+1} \mid s_t, a_t)\)\(a_t = \pi(s_t)\), or a distribution \(\pi(a_t \mid s_t)\)
Describesthe world: physics, contact, the robot's own bodythe agent's behaviour

A model is a map and a physics engine; a policy is the driver. A policy is defined by that job, not by how it was obtained: reinforcement learning, demonstrations (Section 4), a hand-designed controller and a planner run at every step all produce one. In reinforcement learning, models and policies meet in three ways. Model-free reinforcement learning (PPO, SAC) learns the policy from experience and never builds a model. Planning (model-predictive control, tree search) runs the model forward over candidate action sequences at every step, executes the first action of the best one, and plans again; the policy is implicit, the model plus an optimiser. Learning in imagination (Dreamer, MBPO) learns a model, then trains a policy on rollouts the model generates. The policy is then amortised planning: a fast reflex that replaces an expensive search.

Put a policy inside a model and you get a new dynamical system with no inputs, \(s_{t+1} = f(s_t, \pi(s_t))\), the closed loop. Stability, passivity and safety are properties of this closed loop, not of the policy alone (passivity, policies as dynamical systems). One caveat on words: in machine learning at large, "model" means any trained network, so a policy network is "a model" too. In control and reinforcement learning, "the model" means the dynamics, and sometimes the reward.

2. Forward models, and why their errors compound

A forward model maps a state and an action to the next state. It is what a simulator computes from physics (dynamics algorithms) and what a learned model fits from data. I trained one on a pendulum, \(\ddot\theta = -g\sin\theta - 0.1\,\dot\theta + u\) with unit mass and length, torque \(|u| \le 2\) N·m and a 0.05 s step: 20,000 random transitions, a two-layer tanh network of width 64, Adam for 20,000 steps. The same network was asked to predict either the next state or the change of state, a choice Nagabandi et al. (2018) recommend.

One step ahead, over fresh random states, predicting the next state gave a root-mean-square angle error of 0.0027 rad; predicting the change gave 0.00034 rad, 8 times less. The state changes by only 0.25 rad per step, so a network that outputs the next state spends its accuracy copying the input, and its error is a fraction of the state rather than of the change.

A model is used by feeding its predictions back in. Over 200 open-loop rollouts of 5 s under random torques, the change model's median angle error grew from 0.00013 rad after one step to 0.0050 rad (0.29°) after 100; the next-state model's reached 0.077 rad (4.4°) (Figure 1). This pendulum is damped and stable, so errors grow roughly in proportion to the horizon. In an unstable system, a balancing robot or a contact that switches on and off, they grow exponentially.

I trained four more change models on bootstrap resamples of the data and rolled out all five. From half a second on, their median disagreement stayed within 35% of the median error, and across rollouts at 2 s the two had a rank correlation of 0.73: the rollouts where the members disagree are the ones that are wrong. PETS (Chua et al. 2018) plans through such an ensemble, and MBPO (Janner et al. 2019) keeps imagined rollouts short for the same reason. A planner is an optimiser, and an optimiser finds the actions where the model is wrong in its favour. Ensembles, short horizons and replanning at every step are the defences.

3. Inverse models: from where to how

An inverse model runs the other way: given where the system is and where it should be next, which action gets it there? In robotics this is classical inverse dynamics, \(\tau = M(q)\ddot q + C(q,\dot q)\dot q + g(q)\), computed by the recursive Newton–Euler algorithm and used as the feedforward in computed-torque control (dynamics algorithms). Learned, it is a network \((s_t, s_{t+1}) \mapsto a_t\), with three common uses.

  • Labelling actions. VPT (Baker et al. 2022) trained an inverse model on about 2,000 hours of Minecraft play with recorded keyboard and mouse actions, used it to label about 70,000 hours of online video, and trained a policy on the result. An inverse model is easier to learn than a policy because it sees the future.
  • Video as a plan. UniPi (Du et al. 2023) generates a video of the task being done and lets an inverse model read the actions off consecutive frames.
  • Features. The intrinsic curiosity module (Pathak et al. 2017) trains features to predict the action between two frames, so they keep what the agent controls and drop what it does not, like leaves moving in the wind.

A forward map is a function; its inverse need not be. Redundant joints, saturated motors and contact all send different actions to the same next state, and then an inverse model trained with squared error returns the average of those actions. The cleanest case is contact. I put a 1 kg block on a frictionless line next to a wall, applied forces up to ±5 N for 0.05 s, and placed half the samples at rest against the wall. Any push into the wall leaves the block exactly where it was. The inverse model, the same network as before with positions and velocities as inputs, recovered the force in free space to 0.025 N and a pull away from the wall to 0.17 N. For pushes it answered −2.47 N every time, the mean of all pushes in the data, with an error of 1.41 N, close to 1.44 N, the standard deviation of a uniform force on [−5, 0] (Figure 2, left). Given the wall's force reading as a fifth input, every error fell below 0.005 N (right).

This is the situation in contact-rich manipulation. How hard a person pressed a peg into its hole while demonstrating leaves almost no trace in the motion, so an inverse model or a behaviour-cloning policy trained on motion alone cannot recover it. It has to be measured, with a force-torque sensor or joint torques, or controlled indirectly through stiffness (variable impedance). An inverse model is also close to a policy: a goal-conditioned policy \(\pi(a \mid s, g)\) whose goal is one step away is an inverse model. The inverse model handles goals it can reach in one step; the policy handles goals it cannot.

4. Imitation learning in the same terms

None of this is specific to reinforcement learning. Behaviour cloning, ACT (Zhao et al. 2023), Diffusion Policy (Chi et al. 2023) and vision-language-action models such as RT-2, OpenVLA and \(\pi_0\) are policies, \(\pi(a \mid o)\), conditioned on images, joint states, history or language and trained by supervised learning on observation–action pairs from demonstrations. Most of them never predict what the world will do, so in the terms of Section 1 they are model-free. Two things blur the picture. Diffusion Policy is a generative model, but what it models is the distribution of actions given observations, so it is a policy; "model" there is meant in the machine-learning sense. And ACT and Diffusion Policy output a chunk of future actions, \(a_t, \dots, a_{t+k}\), rather than one. That is still a policy, which runs open loop for the length of the chunk.

Models do enter imitation learning, in several places:

And if a pipeline replays, augments or checks demonstrations in simulation, the simulator is the model.

Behaviour cloning also inherits the failure of Section 2. A forward model's predictions become its next inputs; a policy's actions decide its next observation. A small action error takes the robot to a state the demonstrator never visited, where the policy has no data and errs more. Ross and Bagnell (2010) showed that the resulting cost can grow with the square of the horizon, and DAgger (Ross et al. 2011) brings it back to linear: roll out the learner, ask the expert to label the states it actually visits, and retrain on everything so far. I tested this on the simplest case I could think of, steering a vehicle at 1 m/s around a circle of radius 2 m. The expert is a smooth feedback law on the distance from the circle and the heading error; the learner sees only position and heading, through the same network as before. Demonstrations start within 2 cm of the path, as a careful demonstrator's would, so they show what to do on the path and almost nothing about getting back to it. Tests start up to 20 cm away. With five demonstrations (1,500 labelled states), behaviour cloning ended 0.13–0.34 m from the circle over the last 10 s, across three seeds; with one to three demonstrations, seven of nine runs ended more than a metre off, having left the circle altogether. DAgger started from one demonstration and added one rollout of its own per round. After two rounds (900 labelled states) it matched the expert's 0.007 m in every seed (Figure 3).

The fixes are the same for policies and for models: train on the states the learner reaches (DAgger for a policy, on-policy data for a model), and shorten the horizon over which errors can grow (action chunks, which ACT's authors motivate by compounding error; short imagined rollouts in MBPO). A third is specific to demonstrations: disturb the demonstrator while recording, so the data contain recoveries (DART, Laskey et al. 2017). The demonstrations above had none.

Finally, a policy need not be learned. An impedance controller is a policy, and so are computed torque and a PD loop. In my contact-skills work the learned policy commands a motion and a stiffness, and an impedance controller turns them into joint torques: a stack of two policies, the learned one acting through the designed one (variable impedance). "The action" is whatever crosses the interface you choose, and the interface decides what has to be learned.

5. Neural dynamics

"Neural dynamics" is an umbrella term for a dynamics model whose right-hand side is a neural network, usually \(s_{t+1} = s_t + \mathrm{NN}_\theta(s_t, a_t)\), as in Section 2. Training is supervised regression on recorded transitions. Much of the research is about building physics into the network. Deep Lagrangian Networks (Lutter et al. 2019) learn the mass matrix, through its Cholesky factor, and the potential energy, and assemble the manipulator equation from them, so the mass matrix stays positive definite. Hamiltonian neural networks (Greydanus et al. 2019) learn an energy and conserve it by construction. Graph networks (Sanchez-Gonzalez et al. 2020) pass messages between particles or bodies and generalise to scenes larger than any they were trained on. And a residual model keeps an analytic simulator and learns only its error.

In neuroscience the same phrase means something else: how the activity of a population of neurons evolves in time. LFADS (Pandarinath et al. 2018) fits a recurrent network whose state reproduces recorded spikes, and motor cortex during reaching looks like a dynamical system whose initial condition is set during preparation (population dynamics). The mathematics is the same; the subject is the brain.

6. Neural ODEs: dynamics in continuous time

A residual block, \(x_{k+1} = x_k + f(x_k)\) (He et al. 2016), is one Euler step of an ordinary differential equation. Chen et al. (2018) took the step to zero:

$$\frac{dx}{dt} = f_\theta(x(t), u(t)), \qquad x(t_1) = \mathrm{ODESolve}\big(f_\theta,\ x(t_0),\ t_0,\ t_1\big).$$

The network learns the vector field, and a numerical solver turns it into a trajectory. Gradients come either by backpropagating through the solver's steps or by the adjoint method, which solves a second ODE backwards and needs memory independent of the number of steps. The model can be queried at any time, which suits irregularly sampled data (Latent ODEs, Rubanova et al. 2019), and physical systems, which really are ODEs. The costs: each step needs several network evaluations, stiff dynamics force tiny steps, and trajectories of an ODE cannot cross, which limits what it can represent until extra dimensions are added (Dupont et al. 2019). Neural controlled differential equations (Kidger et al. 2020) let an input path drive the dynamics.

The test that matters is time. I sampled 6,000 pairs of states of a damped pendulum separated by random intervals between 0.02 and 0.10 s. A discrete model received the interval as a third input and predicted the change. A neural ODE learned the vector field and was trained by backpropagating through one fourth-order Runge–Kutta step per interval. Both used the same network, data, loss and optimiser. Inside the training range both were accurate: median relative errors of 0.11% for the neural ODE and 0.16–1.4% for the discrete model, worst at the shortest steps (Figure 4). Outside it they parted. At 0.4 s the discrete model was off by 81% and the neural ODE by 0.075%. At 0.005 s the discrete model was off by 49%: it never learned that no time means no change, which an ODE satisfies by construction.

In a neural ODE, time is the solver's job, not the network's. That is why it can be trained on data logged at one rate and run at another, or on a log with dropped samples. The price here was 64 network evaluations for the 0.4 s step where the discrete model needed one.

7. State-space models, first meaning: a hidden state

In control and statistics, a state-space model has two parts:

$$x_{t+1} = f(x_t, u_t) + w_t \quad\text{(transition)}, \qquad y_t = g(x_t) + v_t \quad\text{(observation)}.$$

The state \(x\) is hidden. It summarises the past so that the future depends on nothing earlier (the Markov property); what you measure is \(y\), through noise \(v\), while the unknown disturbance \(w\) pushes the state. When \(f\) and \(g\) are linear, \(x_{t+1} = Ax_t + Bu_t\) and \(y_t = Cx_t + Du_t\), and with Gaussian noise the Kalman filter (Kalman 1960) computes the best estimate of \(x\) online: predict with the model, then correct by the measurement, weighting each by its uncertainty. A hidden Markov model is the same structure with a discrete state.

A robot joint under impedance control behaves like a mass on a spring and damper. I simulated one (1 kg, 40 N/m, 1 N·s/m), pushed it with an unknown random force, and measured only its position, at 100 Hz with 2 mm of noise. Its velocity had a standard deviation of 0.23 m/s. Differencing the positions gave a velocity error of 0.28 m/s, more than the velocity itself. Low-pass filtering the difference, with the filter constant tuned to its best, gave 0.077 m/s, and the Kalman filter 0.040 m/s (Figure 5). The filter wins by knowing the dynamics, so its advantage depends on how predictable they are: with four times the random force, it was 1.5 times better than the tuned low-pass instead of 1.9.

Learned world models are state-space models. PlaNet and Dreamer (Hafner et al. 2019, 2020) use a recurrent state-space model: neural networks for \(f\) and \(g\), a latent state with a deterministic and a stochastic part, and pixels as \(y\). The encoder plays the role of the filter's correction step, the transition the prediction step, and the policy acts on the latent state. This framing is the right one whenever the robot cannot see its true state, which with cameras is always.

8. State-space models, second meaning: S4 and Mamba

Since 2021 the phrase has also named a family of sequence layers that compete with Transformers. They use the linear system \(\dot x = Ax + Bu\), \(y = Cx + Du\) as a layer, where \(u\) is a sequence of features such as tokens, audio or sensor readings, not a control. A step size \(\Delta\) turns it into a recurrence; with a zero-order hold, \(\bar A = e^{\Delta A}\), \(\bar B = A^{-1}(e^{\Delta A} - I)B\), and \(x_k = \bar A x_{k-1} + \bar B u_k\). Because the system is linear and time-invariant, the recurrence unrolls into a convolution,

$$y = K * u, \qquad K = \big(C\bar B,\ C\bar A\bar B,\ C\bar A^2\bar B,\ \dots\big),$$

so a layer trains in parallel like a convolution and runs one step at a time like a recurrent network. I computed a diagonal layer (S4D initialisation, 64 modes) on 4,096 steps both ways; the outputs agreed to 9×10−15 relative to their size. S4 (Gu et al. 2022) made this work over long sequences with a structured \(A\) initialised from HiPPO (Gu et al. 2020), whose state stores a running polynomial approximation of the input's history. S4D, S5 and LRU simplified \(A\) to a diagonal (Gu et al. 2022; Smith et al. 2023; Orvieto et al. 2023).

The step \(\Delta\) sets the memory: the mode of an eigenvalue \(\lambda\) of \(A\) decays by \(e^{\Delta\,\mathrm{Re}(\lambda)}\) per step. In a time-invariant layer \(\Delta\) is fixed, so every input is remembered for the same time, whatever it is. Mamba (Gu and Dao 2023) makes \(\Delta\), \(B\) and \(C\) functions of the input. The convolution no longer exists and the layer is computed with a parallel scan, but now a large \(\Delta\) overwrites the state with the current input and a small one ignores the input and keeps the state. With one mode, \(A = -1\) and \(B = 1\), it is exactly a gated recurrence, \(x_k = (1 - g_k)x_{k-1} + g_k u_k\) with \(g_k = 1 - e^{-\Delta_k}\) (their Theorem 1).

I tested what that buys on a task that needs it: sequences of 600 random inputs, with one input marked on average every 60 steps, and the target at every step is the most recently marked value. A time-invariant layer was given only the marked values (the input times the mark) and the best output scale for each fixed \(\Delta\). Its best error was 0.69, against a target of size 1.0 (Figure 6): with slow decay it blends the new mark with old ones, with fast decay it forgets each mark before the next arrives. A selective layer, \(\Delta = 10\) on marked inputs and \(10^{-4}\) otherwise, reached 0.008. It can reset; a fixed kernel cannot.

These layers are architectures, not models of a physical system. They can be the backbone of a world model or a policy, as a Transformer can, and the two meanings meet there: a recurrent policy is a state-space model whose hidden state is its memory.

10. The sensorimotor connection

The nervous system has both kinds of model. A forward model predicts the sensory consequences of a motor command from a copy of it, and is combined with delayed feedback much as a Kalman filter combines prediction and measurement (Wolpert, Ghahramani and Jordan 1995). An inverse model produces the commands for a desired motion, and people relearn it when a force field changes their arm's dynamics (Shadmehr and Mussa-Ivaldi 1994). Both are thought to live partly in the cerebellum (Wolpert, Miall and Kawato 1998; internal models). The wall experiment has a biological counterpart too: the hand does not infer contact forces from motion, it measures them with receptors in the fingertips (touch and grip). And the network in my motor cortex project is a policy that is also a state-space model: trained across many force fields and then frozen, it adapts to a new one from memory alone, by moving its hidden state rather than its weights.

11. Takeaways

  • A model predicts and a policy decides. MPC makes a policy out of a model at run time; Dreamer and MBPO distil one from it.
  • Predict the change of state, use an ensemble, and trust a rollout only as far as the members agree.
  • An inverse model can only recover what the states record. At contact, add force.
  • Behaviour cloning, ACT, Diffusion Policy and VLAs are policies, and their errors compound like a model's. Label the states the policy actually reaches (DAgger), record recoveries, or shorten the horizon with chunks.
  • Use a neural ODE when the time step varies or differs between the data and the robot.
  • "State-space model" means a hidden-state dynamical system in control and a linear sequence layer in deep learning. Check which one a paper means.
