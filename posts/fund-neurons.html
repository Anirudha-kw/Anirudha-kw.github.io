How Neurons Compute: Physics, Energy and Delay

[Fundamentals]

Fundamentals: optimisation · neural networks · floating point · memory and the 1 kHz loop · GPUs · processes and the kernel · dynamics algorithms · deploying policies · policy files · how neurons compute · bits, delays and control. Measured on my workstation (Intel i7-9700, RTX 2070 SUPER, Ubuntu 24.04, Linux 6.8); code in the site's tools/posts/.

A neuron is an electrical circuit: a membrane that acts as a capacitor, and ion channels that act as voltage-controlled conductances. Hodgkin and Huxley's model of it is still the best piece of physics-based system identification in biology. I simulated it to measure three things computing hardware also cares about. First, the energy of a spike: the squid model lets in 13.3 times the minimum sodium charge at its native 6.3 °C and 4.0 times at 18.5 °C. Second, the speed of conduction: 18.8 m/s for the squid giant axon, against Hodgkin and Huxley's own 18.8. Third, how delays grow with body size. I then measured what a GPU pays per operation, for comparison.

1. The membrane as a circuit

Each ion species has an equilibrium (Nernst) potential set by its concentration ratio, \(E = \frac{RT}{zF}\ln\frac{[\text{out}]}{[\text{in}]}\), about +50 mV for sodium and −77 mV for potassium in the squid axon. Ion channels are conductances pulling the voltage toward these potentials, and the membrane is a capacitor between them:

$$C\frac{dV}{dt} = -\bar g_{\text{Na}} m^3h\,(V - E_{\text{Na}}) - \bar g_{\text{K}} n^4 (V - E_{\text{K}}) - g_L (V - E_L) + I .$$

The gating variables \(m, h, n\) relax toward voltage-dependent steady states with voltage-dependent time constants. Hodgkin and Huxley (1952) fitted those functions to voltage-clamp recordings: system identification of a nonlinear dynamical system, done by hand. The model produces the action potential in Figure 1. Sodium channels open fast and the voltage shoots toward \(E_{\text{Na}}\); then inactivation (\(h\)) and slower potassium channels (\(n\)) pull it back.

2. What a spike costs

The least charge that can produce a spike is what it takes to charge the capacitor, \(C\Delta V\). Any sodium entering while potassium is leaving is wasted, and has to be pumped back out by the Na/K pump at one ATP per three sodium ions. In the squid model the overlap is large: at 6.3 °C the sodium charge is 13.3 times the minimum, at 18.5 °C 4.0 times, at 30 °C 1.9 times (Figure 1, right). Faster kinetics shorten the overlap. Mammalian axons are far better; Alle, Roth and Geiger (2009) found hippocampal mossy-fibre spikes close to the minimum. At 18.5 °C the model's 0.39 µC/cm² of sodium needs about 8.1×1011 ATP per square centimetre per spike. At roughly 50 kJ/mol per ATP, a spike along 1 cm of 1 µm axon with these kinetics would cost about 21 pJ. Attwell and Laughlin (2001) estimated that action potentials and synaptic signalling dominate the grey matter's energy budget. The whole brain, about 2% of body mass, uses about 20% of resting energy (Raichle and Gusnard 2002), roughly 20 W.

3. How fast signals travel

Along a bare axon a spike spreads by local currents and regenerates as it goes. Cable theory says the velocity scales as the square root of the diameter. I solved the Hodgkin–Huxley equations on a cable at 18.5 °C (Figure 2, left). The squid giant axon (476 µm) conducts at 18.8 m/s, against the 18.8 m/s Hodgkin and Huxley computed and the 21.2 m/s they measured, and a 25 µm axon at 4.3 m/s. Myelin changes the scaling. Insulation with gaps makes the spike jump between nodes, and velocity grows in proportion to diameter, about 6 m/s per µm of fibre (Hursh 1939; Rushton 1951). A 10 µm myelinated fibre conducts at about 60 m/s; an unmyelinated axon would need to be thousands of times thicker.

Conduction delay therefore grows with body size. More and Donelan (2018) combined electrophysiology, literature review and biophysical models across terrestrial mammals. They found the total delay of the fastest locomotor reflex scales with body mass as \(M^{0.21}\), so large mammals wait about 17 times longer than small ones; slower movements in large animals offset much of that (Figure 2, right). A robot's wiring is not the bottleneck: EtherCAT and CAN deliver messages in microseconds to milliseconds. Its delays come from computing, sensing and the operating system (processes).

4. Energy per operation, measured on a GPU

I sampled the board power of the RTX 2070 SUPER through NVML while it ran sustained work (Figure 3). An fp32 matrix multiply drew 213 W at 8.4 TFLOPS, 25 pJ per floating-point operation; fp16 on the tensor cores drew 213 W at 34.1 TFLOPS, 6.2 pJ per operation. Small work is far worse: a batch-1 MLP forward pass cost 43,809 pJ per operation, because the board burns power while waiting on launches; at batch 4096 the cost fell to 63 pJ. The board idled at 28 W. Operations and spikes are not the same unit, so the comparison is only suggestive. Still, the brain does its sensorimotor work on about 20 W with slow, sparse, noisy elements, and a GPU spends its energy moving data and waiting.

5. Computing like a nervous system

  • Code what is informative. Laughlin (1981) showed that a fly's large monopolar cells map contrast to response with the cumulative distribution of natural contrasts: histogram equalisation, the code that uses the response range evenly. Olshausen and Field (1996) found that learning a sparse code for natural images produces receptive fields like those of primary visual cortex.
  • Compute on change. Event cameras report per-pixel brightness changes asynchronously with microsecond resolution and little redundancy (Gallego et al. 2022). Neuromorphic chips such as Intel's Loihi run spiking networks in which only active units consume energy (Davies et al. 2018). Both suit fast reactive control, where most of the scene does not change most of the time.
  • Accept delay and predict. Slow wiring is compensated by forward models and local reflexes (bits, delays and control).
