Gradient Descent on a Manifold

[Misc]

Many quantities a robot learns are not vectors: unit vectors, rotations, stiffness matrices. Gradient descent on them needs two ideas, the Riemannian gradient (the steepest direction that stays tangent to the constraint set) and a retraction (a way back onto the set after a step). This note derives both, works through the sphere, rotations and SPD matrices, and computes the geometric average of five stiffness matrices, which the ordinary average gets wrong.

1. Gradients that respect a constraint

Let \(\mathcal M\) be a smooth manifold with an inner product \(\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_x\) on each tangent space \(T_x\mathcal M\). The Riemannian gradient of \(f\) is the tangent vector that represents the directional derivative:

$$\mathrm Df(x)[v] = \langle \operatorname{grad} f(x),\, v\rangle_x \quad \text{for all } v \in T_x\mathcal M .$$

For a submanifold of Euclidean space with the inherited inner product this is simply the Euclidean gradient projected onto the tangent space, \(\operatorname{grad} f(x) = P_{T_x\mathcal M}\nabla f(x)\). With a different metric, the representing vector changes: the metric is part of the algorithm.

2. Retractions

A step along the gradient leaves the manifold. A retraction \(\mathcal R_x: T_x\mathcal M \to \mathcal M\) maps it back, with \(\mathcal R_x(0) = x\) and derivative the identity at zero, so it agrees with the exponential map to first order. Riemannian gradient descent is then

$$x_{k+1} = \mathcal R_{x_k}\big(-\eta_k \operatorname{grad} f(x_k)\big).$$

Under the same smoothness assumptions as in the Euclidean case, with a suitable step size, it converges to critical points at the same rates; Absil, Mahony and Sepulchre (2008) and Boumal (2023) give the theory.

3. The sphere: an eigenvector by descent

Minimise the Rayleigh quotient \(f(x) = x^\top A x\) over unit vectors. The Euclidean gradient is \(2Ax\); its tangent projection is

$$\operatorname{grad} f(x) = (I - xx^\top)\,2Ax = 2\big(Ax - (x^\top A x)\,x\big),$$

which vanishes exactly when \(x\) is an eigenvector. Normalisation, \(\mathcal R_x(v) = (x+v)/\|x+v\|\), is a retraction. Descent from a generic start converges to the eigenvector of the smallest eigenvalue, the global minimiser (Figure 1). The construction is general: maximise instead and you have a power method with a step size.

4. Rotations

On \(SO(3)\), tangent vectors at \(R\) are \(R\hat\omega\). Projecting a Euclidean gradient \(G\) gives \(\operatorname{grad} f(R) = R\,\operatorname{skew}(R^\top G)\), with \(\operatorname{skew}(B) = \tfrac12(B - B^\top)\), and the retraction \(R\exp(R^\top V)\) uses the exponential map derived in Rotations Form a Group. Alternatives such as the QR decomposition or the Cayley transform are cheaper retractions with the same first-order behaviour.

5. SPD matrices: averaging stiffness

On SPD matrices with the affine-invariant metric \(\langle U,V\rangle_X = \operatorname{tr}(X^{-1}UX^{-1}V)\), the Riemannian gradient of \(f\) is \(X\operatorname{sym}(\nabla f)X\) and the exponential map is \(\operatorname{Exp}_X(V) = X^{1/2}\exp(X^{-1/2}VX^{-1/2})X^{1/2}\). A natural problem is the mean of several stiffnesses \(K_1,\dots,K_N\), the minimiser of

$$f(X) = \frac{1}{2N}\sum_i d_{\mathrm{AI}}(X, K_i)^2, \qquad -\operatorname{grad} f(X) = \frac1N\sum_i \operatorname{Log}_X(K_i),$$

where \(\operatorname{Log}_X(K) = X^{1/2}\log(X^{-1/2}KX^{-1/2})X^{1/2}\). A unit step along the negative gradient gives the fixed-point iteration

$$X_{k+1} = X_k^{1/2}\exp\Big(\frac1N\sum_i \log\big(X_k^{-1/2}K_iX_k^{-1/2}\big)\Big)X_k^{1/2},$$

which converged linearly here, to a gradient norm of 1e-15 in 25 iterations (Figure 2). The result, the Karcher mean, has determinant equal to the geometric mean of the inputs' determinants (100,155 here), while the arithmetic mean's determinant is 2.59 times larger: the swelling effect explained in Distances Between Stiffness Matrices. No eigenvalue clipping or projection is needed at any step, because every iterate is SPD by construction.

6. The same idea in safe RL

ATACOM (Liu et al.) applies this geometry to actions rather than parameters. Constraints \(c(q) = 0\) define a manifold; the policy outputs a velocity in its tangent space, mapped through a basis of the constraint's null space, and a correction term pulls drift back onto the manifold, a retraction in continuous time. Exploration then never leaves the safe set (Safe RL for Contact).

7. What to take away

  • Project the gradient onto the tangent space, step, retract: three lines turn any constrained-parameter problem on a manifold into gradient descent.
  • The metric is a modelling choice. On SPD matrices the affine-invariant metric makes every iterate positive definite and every average volume-preserving.
  • Learning a rotation, a direction or a stiffness with an unconstrained parameterisation plus normalisation is Riemannian descent with an implicit metric; it is worth knowing which one.
