Distances Between Stiffness Matrices

[Misc]

A stiffness matrix is symmetric positive definite (SPD), and the SPD matrices are not a vector space but an open cone with a natural curved geometry. Straight lines in the cone "swell": halfway between two directional stiffnesses they are stiff in every direction. This note derives why, builds the two metrics that fix it (affine-invariant and log-Euclidean), and shows what each one does to a stiffness schedule, ending with the derivative you need to put a matrix exponential inside a policy.

1. The cone

For 2×2 matrices the picture is exact. Write \(X = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ b & c\end{pmatrix}\) in coordinates \(\tau = \tfrac{a+c}{2}\), \(x = \tfrac{a-c}{2}\), \(y = b\). The eigenvalues are \(\tau \pm \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}\), so \(X \succ 0\) exactly when

$$\tau > \sqrt{x^2 + y^2} ,$$

the inside of a round cone (Figure 1). Its determinant is \(\det X = \tau^2 - x^2 - y^2\), so matrices of equal determinant lie on a hyperboloid sheet inside the cone. Hold that picture: the unit-determinant sheet with the affine-invariant metric is exactly the hyperbolic plane.

2. Why straight lines swell

Averaging stays SPD because the cone is convex, but it does not preserve volume. The function \(\log\det\) is concave on SPD matrices, so for any \(A, B\)

$$\log\det\Big(\frac{A+B}{2}\Big) \;\ge\; \frac{\log\det A + \log\det B}{2}, \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad \det\Big(\frac{A+B}{2}\Big) \ge \sqrt{\det A\,\det B},$$

with equality only when \(A = B\). Concavity itself comes from the second derivative: along \(X + tE\), \(\frac{d^2}{dt^2}\log\det(X+tE) = -\operatorname{tr}\big((X^{-1}E)^2\big) \le 0\). For a stiffness schedule this means the Euclidean midpoint of "stiff along \(x\)" and "stiff along \(y\)" is stiffer than either, in every direction.

3. The affine-invariant metric

The tangent space at any \(X\) is the space of symmetric matrices. Choose the inner product

$$\langle U, V\rangle_X = \operatorname{tr}\big(X^{-1} U X^{-1} V\big).$$

It is invariant under every congruence \(X \mapsto WXW^\top\) with \(W\) invertible, which is what a change of units or of coordinate frame does to a stiffness. That invariance gives the distance almost for free: move \(A\) to the identity with \(W = A^{-1/2}\), so \(B\) becomes \(C = A^{-1/2}BA^{-1/2}\); at the identity the metric is the Frobenius one and the geodesic is \(t\mapsto\exp(tS)\), so

$$d_{\mathrm{AI}}(A,B) = \big\|\log\big(A^{-1/2}BA^{-1/2}\big)\big\|_F = \Big(\sum_i \log^2\lambda_i\big(A^{-1}B\big)\Big)^{1/2}, \qquad \gamma(t) = A^{1/2}\big(A^{-1/2}BA^{-1/2}\big)^{t}A^{1/2}.$$

The distance depends only on the eigenvalues of \(A^{-1}B\), the generalised eigenvalues: how many times stiffer \(B\) is than \(A\) along each of their joint principal directions. Every SPD matrix sits infinitely far from the boundary, so no geodesic can cross into indefinite stiffness.

4. The log-Euclidean metric

The matrix logarithm is a bijection from SPD matrices onto all symmetric matrices. Pull the flat geometry back through it:

$$d_{\mathrm{LE}}(A,B) = \|\log A - \log B\|_F, \qquad \gamma(t) = \exp\big((1-t)\log A + t\log B\big).$$

Means become closed-form (\(\exp\) of the mean log), and the determinant along every geodesic interpolates geometrically because \(\log\det = \operatorname{tr}\log\). The price is invariance: only rotations and uniform scaling leave \(d_{\mathrm{LE}}\) unchanged, not general congruences. When \(A\) and \(B\) commute the two geodesics coincide; when their principal axes differ they do not (Figure 2).

5. What each geometry does to a schedule

Figure 3 tracks volume and anisotropy along the paths. The Euclidean path inflates the determinant; both Riemannian geodesics keep it. But none of the three keeps the shape. Between two rotated copies of the same 10:1 stiffness, every geodesic passes through a much rounder matrix, because shrinking the anisotropy is a shorter way to change the matrix than turning it. The path that keeps the shape, \(R(t)AR(t)^\top\), is a circle on the constant-determinant sheet of Figure 1, not a geodesic of any of these metrics.

That has a design consequence. If a task needs the same compliance in a turning frame, say stiff along a tool axis that rotates, interpolating stiffness in any of these geometries softens it mid-turn. Parameterising the frame and the eigenvalues separately, \(K = R\,\mathrm{diag}(k)\,R^\top\), keeps the shape by construction. A policy that outputs a full matrix through \(\exp\) or Cholesky inherits geodesic-like blending, which is right for moving between stiffness regimes and wrong for rotating one.

For these two matrices the distances are \(d_{\mathrm E} = 1229\) N/m, \(d_{\mathrm{LE}} = 3.15\) and \(d_{\mathrm{AI}} = 3.18\). The last two are unitless: a factor of \(e^{d}\) in stiffness, in the sense of the generalised eigenvalues.

6. Putting a matrix exponential in a policy

A policy can output a symmetric \(S\) and use \(K = \exp(S)\): every output is SPD, and linear operations in the network become geometric means in stiffness. Backpropagation needs the derivative of \(\exp\) at a symmetric matrix. With \(S = Q\Lambda Q^\top\), the Daleckii–Krein formula gives

$$D\exp(S)[E] = Q\,\big(\Gamma \circ (Q^\top E Q)\big)\,Q^\top, \qquad \Gamma_{ij} = \begin{cases} \dfrac{e^{\lambda_i} - e^{\lambda_j}}{\lambda_i - \lambda_j}, & \lambda_i \ne \lambda_j \\[4pt] e^{\lambda_i}, & \lambda_i = \lambda_j \end{cases}$$

where \(\circ\) is the elementwise product. The same divided-difference structure applies to \(\log\) and to matrix powers. The case split is exactly where naive code fails: an isotropic stiffness has repeated eigenvalues, and the first branch becomes \(0/0\). Use the second branch, or its Taylor expansion, whenever \(|\lambda_i - \lambda_j|\) is small. The alternative is a Cholesky output \(K = LL^\top\) with a positive diagonal, which is cheaper and needs no eigendecomposition, but whose straight lines are neither of the two geometries above.

7. What to take away

  • Interpolating or averaging stiffness entrywise inflates it. Use log-Euclidean when you need speed and closed-form means, affine-invariant when frame and unit invariance matter.
  • No metric interpolation preserves anisotropy through a rotation. If the frame turns, parameterise the frame.
  • Distances between stiffnesses are about ratios, the generalised eigenvalues of \(A^{-1}B\), not differences in N/m.
  • The derivative of \(\exp\) at an isotropic matrix is a \(0/0\) waiting to happen; handle repeated eigenvalues explicitly.
