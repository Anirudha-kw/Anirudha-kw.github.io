Optimal Feedback Control and the Minimum-Intervention Principle

[Literature Review]

Part of a series on sensorimotor neuroscience and robot learning: impedance · internal models · optimal feedback control · touch and grip · synergies and primitives · population dynamics.

Why are reaching movements smooth and straight with bell-shaped speed? Why does variability pile up in some directions and not others? A sequence of optimal-control theories answered with increasing precision: minimum jerk, minimum endpoint variance under signal-dependent noise, and finally optimal feedback control, which predicts that the nervous system corrects only the errors that matter. The last of these became a design principle for robot controllers and a reason MuJoCo exists.

1. Smoothness: minimum jerk

Flash and Hogan (1985) proposed that point-to-point hand paths minimise the integrated squared jerk, \(\int \|\dddot x\|^2 dt\). With rest-to-rest boundary conditions the optimum is a fifth-order polynomial in normalised time \(s = t/T\),

$$x(s) = x_0 + (x_f - x_0)\,(10 s^3 - 15 s^4 + 6 s^5),$$

a straight path with a symmetric bell-shaped speed profile (Figure 1). It matched measured reaches well, but it is a kinematic cost: it says nothing about why the brain would care about jerk.

2. A reason for smoothness: signal-dependent noise

Harris and Wolpert (1998) replaced the kinematic cost with a physiological one. Motor noise grows with the size of the motor command, so large commands make movements less accurate. Choosing the command sequence that minimises endpoint variance, for a given movement duration, predicts smooth bell-shaped trajectories and the speed–accuracy trade-off: faster movements need larger commands, hence more noise, hence larger errors. The cost now has a reason, and it is about the outcome rather than the path.

3. Optimal feedback control and minimum intervention

Both theories plan a trajectory and assume it is then executed. Todorov and Jordan (2002) dropped the trajectory: the controller is the optimal feedback law for a cost on the task outcome and on effort, under noise. For linear dynamics, quadratic cost and Gaussian noise this is LQG control,

$$\min_{\pi} \; \mathbb E\Big[(c^\top x_T - g)^2\,w_T + \sum_t u_t^\top R\, u_t\Big], \qquad u_t = -L_t\,\hat x_t .$$

Its signature prediction is the minimum-intervention principle: deviations that do not affect the task are left alone, because correcting them costs effort and adds noise without improving the outcome. Variability should therefore pile up along task-irrelevant directions, the structure Scholz and Schöner (1999) had measured and called the uncontrolled manifold. Figure 2 shows it in a two-effector task where only the sum \(x_1 + x_2\) matters.

4. The neural evidence and the reviews

Scott (2004) argued that optimal feedback control is the right framework for interpreting the neural basis of voluntary movement, primary motor cortex included, because feedback in it is flexible and task-dependent rather than a fixed reflex. Todorov (2004) reviewed how optimality principles, with noise and feedback included, account for a broad set of motor phenomena.

5. Into robotics

  • iLQR. Li and Todorov (2004) introduced the iterative linear quadratic regulator to apply optimal feedback control to nonlinear biological movement models. It is now a workhorse of trajectory optimisation and model-predictive control for robots.
  • MuJoCo. Todorov, Erez and Tassa (IROS 2012) built MuJoCo as a physics engine for model-based control. It is now one of the main simulators for robot learning, and the one most of my own experiments run on.
  • Null-space and task-space control. Operational-space controllers act in the task space and leave the redundant null space to a secondary objective, a hand-designed minimum intervention.

6. What it says about RL objectives

  • Reward outcomes, not paths. Tracking rewards on a reference trajectory are servo controllers: they spend effort correcting irrelevant deviations and are brittle when the reference is wrong. Rewards on task outcomes plus an effort cost let the policy find the minimum-intervention solution itself.
  • Model noise as signal-dependent. Exploration and actuator noise that scales with the command is both more realistic and what makes smoothness emerge without a jerk penalty.
  • Expect structured variability. A well-trained policy should be variable in task-irrelevant directions. Low variance everywhere is not a sign of a better policy; it may be a sign of a servo.
