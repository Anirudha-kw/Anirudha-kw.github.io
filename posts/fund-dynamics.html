Rigid-Body Dynamics as an Algorithm

[Fundamentals]

Fundamentals: optimisation · neural networks · floating point · memory and the 1 kHz loop · GPUs · processes and the kernel · dynamics algorithms · deploying policies · policy files · how neurons compute · bits, delays and control. Measured on my workstation (Intel i7-9700, RTX 2070 SUPER, Ubuntu 24.04, Linux 6.8); code in the site's tools/posts/.

Physics simulators, model-based controllers and system identification all spend their time in a few recursions over the robot's kinematic tree. Featherstone's spatial-vector algorithms compute inverse dynamics and forward dynamics in time linear in the number of joints, and the mass matrix in quadratic time. I implemented all three in NumPy, checked them against MuJoCo to \(10^{-13}\), and timed them. Python costs about 63 µs per link; MuJoCo's C does a full forward-dynamics pass for 8 links in 2.6 µs. Physics also sets the time step: contact stiffness bounds it, which is why simulators soften contact. On the GPU, MJX steps 994,271 Panda environments per second at batch 4,096, about 2.1 times MuJoCo on 8 CPU threads.

1. What has to be computed

A manipulator obeys

$$M(q)\,\ddot q + C(q,\dot q)\,\dot q + g(q) = \tau + J(q)^\top f_{\text{ext}} ,$$

derived from the Lagrangian in Why Coriolis Forces Do No Work. Inverse dynamics asks for \(\tau\) given \(\ddot q\), as in computed-torque control and feedforward. Forward dynamics asks for \(\ddot q\) given \(\tau\), as in simulation. Writing out \(M\) and \(C\) symbolically is hopeless beyond a few links. The efficient route is recursive. Describe each body's velocity, acceleration and force as 6-D spatial vectors (angular and linear parts together), move them between frames with 6×6 Plücker transforms \(X\), and sweep along the tree (Featherstone 2008).

2. Three recursions

RNEA, the recursive Newton–Euler algorithm (Luh, Walker and Paul 1980), does inverse dynamics in two passes. Outward from the base, each body's velocity and acceleration follow from its parent's and its joint's motion \(S\dot q_i\). Inward from the leaves, each body's net force is passed to its parent and projected onto the joint axis:

$$\begin{aligned} v_i &= X_i v_{\lambda(i)} + S_i\dot q_i, & a_i &= X_i a_{\lambda(i)} + S_i\ddot q_i + v_i\times S_i\dot q_i,\\ f_i &= I_i a_i + v_i\times^{*} I_i v_i, & \tau_i &= S_i^\top f_i, \quad f_{\lambda(i)} \mathrel{+}= X_i^\top f_i . \end{aligned}$$

Gravity enters as a fictitious upward acceleration of the base. The cost is \(O(n)\). CRBA, the composite rigid-body algorithm (Walker and Orin 1982), builds \(M(q)\) by treating each subtree as one rigid body, \(I^c_{\lambda(i)} \mathrel{+}= X_i^\top I^c_i X_i\), then reading off \(M_{ij}\) by projecting through the chain, at \(O(n^2)\). Forward dynamics then needs a solve with \(M\), \(O(n^3)\) for a dense matrix. ABA, the articulated-body algorithm (Featherstone 1983), avoids forming \(M\) at all. It carries each subtree's articulated inertia inward, \(I^A_{\lambda(i)} \mathrel{+}= X_i^\top\big(I^A_i - U_iU_i^\top/d_i\big)X_i\) with \(U_i = I^A_iS_i\) and \(d_i = S_i^\top U_i\), then solves for each joint's acceleration on the way out, in \(O(n)\).

3. Checked against MuJoCo, then timed

I implemented all three for a 3-D chain of revolute joints with random link lengths, twists and box-shaped inertias (tools/posts/rbd.py), and wrote the same chain as MJCF for MuJoCo. For 7 links, the mass matrix from CRBA matches MuJoCo's to 1×10−15, the bias forces from RNEA to 1×10−14, and the accelerations from ABA to 1×10−13. CRBA also matches the columns of \(M\) obtained by calling RNEA with unit accelerations. Figure 1 times them against the number of links.

The NumPy versions show the predicted scaling, linear for RNEA and ABA and quadratic for CRBA, at roughly 63 µs per link: almost all of it is Python interpreting small 6×6 operations. MuJoCo does far more per call (kinematics, Jacobians, the mass matrix, its factorisation, bias forces, constraint setup) in 2.6 µs for 8 links and 27 µs for 64. It is about two hundred times faster at small \(n\). For a long unbranched chain the mass matrix is dense and its factorisation grows like \(n^3\). Branched robots such as humanoids and hands keep it sparse, which is what MuJoCo exploits.

4. Integration: physics sets the time step

A simulator advances these equations in discrete steps, and stability is a property of the integrator and the stiffest part of the system. For an undamped oscillator \(\ddot x = -\omega^2 x\) with step \(\Delta t\), each method multiplies the amplitude per step by a factor that depends only on \(h = \omega\Delta t\) (Figure 2, left). Explicit Euler, \(\sqrt{1+h^2}\), always grows. Semi-implicit (symplectic) Euler keeps the energy error bounded up to \(h = 2\) and then explodes. Fourth-order Runge–Kutta is stable to \(h = 2\sqrt2\) with slight damping. Implicit Euler is stable at any step but damps the motion away. Symplectic integrators, which MuJoCo's default resembles for the smooth dynamics, are the reason long simulations do not gain or lose energy steadily (Hairer, Lubich and Wanner 2006).

Contact turns this into a hard limit. A contact modelled as a spring of stiffness \(k\) on a mass \(m\) has \(\omega = \sqrt{k/m}\), so the step must stay below about \(2\sqrt{m/k}\) (Figure 2, right). A 1 kg link on a \(10^6\) N/m contact allows 2 ms. Steel on steel, at \(10^8\)–\(10^9\) N/m, would need tens of microseconds. Simulators therefore make contact softer than reality. MuJoCo describes each contact by a time constant (default 20 ms) and its documentation advises keeping it at least twice the time step. That softness is a deliberate approximation and one source of the sim-to-real gap (adaptation review).

5. Thousands of robots at once

Learning needs throughput, not one fast step. MuJoCo on the CPU steps the Menagerie MJX Panda scene (5 ms time step) at 74,590 steps per second on one thread and 473,258 with mujoco.rollout across 8 threads. MJX, MuJoCo reimplemented in JAX, runs the same scene on the GPU. One environment at a time it is slow, 1,573 steps per second, because every step launches GPU kernels. Batched, it reaches 994,271 at 4,096 environments (Figure 3), about 2.1 times MuJoCo on all eight CPU cores. Each environment steps at only 243 per second, but there are thousands of them (GPU note). The MJX documentation is explicit that contact-rich scenes need simplified collision geometry to run well on the GPU, which is why the Menagerie ships separate MJX models. On a larger GPU or a simpler scene the balance shifts further toward the GPU; on this card and this contact scene the gain is real but modest.

6. The sensorimotor connection

The cerebellum is thought to hold internal models of exactly these maps. When Shadmehr and Mussa-Ivaldi (1994) added a velocity-dependent force field to reaching, people learned to predict it, which amounts to updating their inverse dynamics (internal models). In my neural-network note, a network trained with random feedback weights learned a two-link arm's inverse dynamics to a normalised error below \(10^{-3}\). The brain need not run RNEA to get RNEA's answer. Bodies also exploit the physics that simulators must integrate carefully: passive dynamics, elastic tendons and compliant contact make many movements cheap and stable without fast computation.

7. Takeaways

  • Use the \(O(n)\) recursions; never form \(M^{-1}\) symbolically. Check any dynamics code against a trusted engine on random states, as above.
  • The stiffest spring in the model sets the time step. Softened contact is a modelling choice, not a fact about the world.
  • One environment: CPU. Thousands: GPU, with simplified collision geometry.
