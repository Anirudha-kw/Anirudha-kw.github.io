Learning to Adapt: System Identification for Sim-to-Real

[Literature Review]

Part of a series on robot learning, read against motor neuroscience: variable impedance · adaptation · passivity · safe RL · stiffness geometry · games. The overview is From Sensorimotor Neuroscience to Robot Learning.

A policy that must work on a robot it has not met has three options: be robust to everything (domain randomisation), infer the hidden parameters from its recent history (rapid motor adaptation), or go and measure them (active system identification). Two small experiments show what is at stake. A memoryless gain tuned over a wide parameter range costs four times the oracle, while one that adapts from its own history stays within 5%. And whether a parameter can be identified at all depends on how the robot moves. The motor-learning literature has studied the same trade-offs in people for three decades.

1. The problem

Write the robot and its environment as a family of dynamics indexed by a hidden parameter \(\theta\) (mass, friction, stiffness, latency), fixed during an episode and unknown to the policy: \(s_{t+1} \sim P(\cdot \mid s_t, a_t; \theta)\). A robust policy \(\pi(a\mid s)\) must do well for every \(\theta\) it was trained on. An adaptive policy \(\pi(a \mid s, h_t)\) also sees the history \(h_t = (s_{0:t}, a_{0:t-1})\), which carries information about \(\theta\). The Bayes-optimal policy acts on the belief \(b_t(\theta) = p(\theta \mid h_t)\), which trades exploring against exploiting (VariBAD, Zintgraf et al., ICLR 2020). Chen et al. (ICLR 2022) proved that under domain randomisation, policies with memory are essential: without history, the sim-to-real gap does not shrink as training improves.

2. Why randomisation makes a memoryless policy conservative

The smallest example is a scalar plant \(x_{t+1} = x_t + b\,u_t + w_t\) with unknown gain \(b\) and a linear policy \(u = -kx\). The closed loop is stable only if \(|1 - bk| < 1\), so a single gain must satisfy \(k < 2/b_{\max}\). If \(b\) is randomised over a wide range, the gain is pinned by the strongest plant and is far too timid for the weak ones. Figure 1 computes this exactly: tuned over \(b\) within a factor of 2, the memoryless gain costs 1.13 times the oracle; within a factor of 4, 1.70; within 10, 4.3. A policy that estimates \(b\) by least squares from its own inputs and state changes, and picks the gain from the estimate, stays within 5% of the oracle even at the widest range, including the time it spends learning.

DORAEMON (Tiboni et al., ICLR 2024) attacks the same problem from the other side: grow the randomisation distribution's entropy only while the success rate stays above a threshold, so the policy is not made timid by parameters it could never handle.

3. Implicit adaptation: rapid motor adaptation

RMA (Kumar et al., RSS 2021) has two phases. First, a base policy \(\pi(a \mid s, z)\) is trained with RL in simulation, where \(z = \mu(e)\) encodes privileged environment parameters \(e\) (friction, payload, terrain). Second, an adaptation module \(\phi\) learns to regress the same latent from the last half second or so of states and actions, \(\hat z_t = \phi(s_{t-k:t}, a_{t-k-1:t-1})\), with supervised learning on the policy's own rollouts. At deployment the base policy runs on \(\hat z\), and the latent updates every step. The same recipe rotates objects in a multi-fingered hand from proprioception alone (Qi et al., CoRL 2022) and infers object properties for arm manipulation (Liang, Ellis and Henriques, CVPR 2024). It descends from the privileged teacher–student distillation of Lee et al. (Science Robotics 2020). DexCtrl (2025) extends it to controller gains: the policy outputs its own gains, conditioned on history.

4. Explicit identification is an experiment

The alternative is to measure \(\theta\). Whether that works depends on the data. Take a block pushed by the robot, with force \(F = m\,a + \mu m g\,\mathrm{sgn}(v)\). The model is linear in \((m, \mu m)\), so least squares applies, and the Fisher information is \(\mathcal I = A^\top A/\sigma^2\) with rows \(A_t = (a_t,\ g\,\mathrm{sgn}\,v_t)\). If the robot pushes at constant speed, \(a_t \equiv 0\) and the mass column vanishes. If it pushes at constant acceleration, the two columns are proportional. In both cases only one combination of mass and friction is identifiable, and the estimates smear along a curve (Figure 2). An oscillating push makes \(A\) full rank and identifies the mass to ±0.04 kg. Swevers et al. (1997) turned this into optimal excitation trajectories for identifying robot dynamics. My first real stiffness-identification demo failed for exactly this reason: one-dimensional excitation, rank-deficient data.

ASID (Memmel et al., ICLR 2024) makes the experiment a policy. It trains an exploration policy in simulation to maximise the Fisher information about the physical parameters, runs it once on the real robot, identifies the parameters, and trains the task policy in the corrected simulator (Figure 3). Poke and Strike (Aoyama et al., CoRL 2025) rewards exploration by how sensitive the task policy is to the estimation error, then switches to the task once the uncertainty no longer matters.

5. Identify what matters for the task

Matching dynamics and maximising task return are different objectives once the simulator cannot represent reality exactly. AdaptSim (Ren et al., CoRL 2023) meta-learns a policy that updates the simulator's parameter distribution from real task outcomes. Figure 4 shows the consequence. On a double pendulum, a Bayesian system-identification baseline finds parameters closer to the true ones, but AdaptSim's parameters, further away, give higher reward. With an irreducible sim-to-real gap, the "wrong" parameters can be the right surrogate.

6. The sensorimotor connection

People solve the same problem every time they pick up an unfamiliar object, and the motor-learning literature (Internal Models and Two-Speed Adaptation) has measured how.

  • Identification by contact events. In a precision grip, the grip-to-load force ratio adjusts to the friction of the surface within about 0.1 s of touching it (Johansson and Westling 1984). The nervous system does not run a separate exploration phase; it identifies from the first informative event and keeps a safety margin while it does (Touch and Grip). Robots rarely use contact onset as an identification moment.
  • Two speeds. Adaptation to a force field is fit by a fast process that learns and forgets quickly and a slow one that retains (Smith, Ghazizadeh and Shadmehr 2006). RMA has the same split: the adaptation module updates \(\hat z\) every step, and the base policy's weights change only in training.
  • Beliefs, not point estimates. Körding and Wolpert (2004) showed that people combine a learned prior over perturbations with noisy feedback in proportion to their reliabilities, as a Bayesian observer would. That is the belief-conditioned policy of VariBAD, measured in humans.
  • Adaptive learning rates. Herzfeld et al. (2014) found that people learn faster from errors in an environment whose perturbations are consistent and slower when they flip. They keep a memory of errors and tune their sensitivity to it. In robot terms the forgetting factor of the estimator is itself learned from how fast the parameters drift.
  • Many models. MOSAIC (Wolpert and Kawato 1998) weights paired forward and inverse models by how well each predicts. It is a mixture of experts with prediction-error gating, close to multiple-model adaptive control.

7. What to take away

  • Randomisation without memory buys robustness by giving up performance, and the price grows quickly with the range. History buys most of it back.
  • Identifiability is a property of the data the robot chooses to collect. Check the rank before trusting an estimate.
  • Identify what changes the optimal action, and use contact events as free experiments.
