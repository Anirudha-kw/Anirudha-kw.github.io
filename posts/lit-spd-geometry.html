Stiffness Lives on a Manifold

[Literature Review]

Part of a series on robot learning, read against motor neuroscience: variable impedance · adaptation · passivity · safe RL · stiffness geometry · games. The overview is From Sensorimotor Neuroscience to Robot Learning.

A stiffness matrix is symmetric positive definite, and the set of such matrices is a curved cone, not a vector space. Robotics has learned stiffness and manipulability on that manifold for a decade, and generative models now run on manifolds too. I trained the same flow-matching model on stiffness matrices in two coordinate systems: in raw matrix entries 4% of its samples were not valid stiffnesses, in the log chart none were. I found no paper showing a learned full SPD stiffness beating a rotated diagonal one.

1. The geometry

The SPD matrices form an open convex cone: positive combinations stay inside, differences need not. For \(2\times2\) matrices the slice of unit trace is a disk. Writing \(u = (k_{11}-k_{22})/\mathrm{tr}\,K\) and \(w = 2k_{12}/\mathrm{tr}\,K\), \(K\) is positive definite exactly when \(u^2 + w^2 < 1\), with the boundary at singular matrices and the centre at the isotropic one. Two metrics dominate. The affine-invariant one (Pennec, Fillard and Ayache 2006) and the log-Euclidean one (Arsigny et al. 2007),

$$d_{\text{AI}}(A,B) = \big\lVert \log\!\big(A^{-1/2} B A^{-1/2}\big)\big\rVert_F, \qquad d_{\text{LE}}(A,B) = \lVert \log A - \log B \rVert_F ,$$

both push singular matrices infinitely far away. Their geodesics stay inside the cone however far they are extended, where a straight line leaves it (Figure 1). The derivations and the swelling of straight-line interpolation are in Distances Between Stiffness Matrices.

2. Learning on the manifold in robotics

Abu-Dakka et al. (RAS 2018) learn full stiffness matrices from demonstration variance and sensed force with mixture models on the SPD manifold; geometry-aware movement primitives (ICRA 2020) let stiffness trajectories evolve on it. Jaquier et al. learn and transfer manipulability ellipsoids (IJRR 2021) and tune impedance with geometry-aware Bayesian optimisation (CoRL 2019; NeurIPS 2020 for high dimensions). Their ICRA 2024 paper is a warning about a common shortcut: map every matrix to one tangent space, then use ordinary machine learning. The log-Euclidean chart is exactly such a tangent space, at the identity. Figure 2 measures what it distorts. For pairs of \(3\times3\) matrices whose logarithms differ by 0.3, the tangent-space distance is within 0.2% of the true affine-invariant distance near the base point. At a geodesic distance of 3 from it, where the eigenvalues span more than an order of magnitude, it understates the true distance by up to 30%.

Whether that matters depends on the range. A stiffness spanning a decade per axis sits within a geodesic distance of about 2 of the identity once it is normalised by its geometric mean, where the worst pairs are already off by 10–20%. For a model that only needs to produce valid matrices it is harmless. For one that averages, interpolates or regresses distances, it is not.

3. Generative models on manifolds

Flow matching (Lipman et al., ICLR 2023) learns a velocity field \(v_\theta(x,t)\) by regressing, along straight paths \(x_t = (1-t)x_0 + t x_1\) from noise to data, onto the path's velocity \(x_1 - x_0\), then generates by integrating the field. Riemannian flow matching (Chen and Lipman, ICLR 2024) replaces straight lines with geodesics, or with paths defined by a premetric when geodesics are expensive, so the conditional field transports every point onto the data point by \(t = 1\) (Figure 3). DiffeoCFM (Collas et al., NeurIPS 2025) takes the cheaper route for matrix manifolds: pull the metric back through a global diffeomorphism such as the matrix logarithm, run ordinary flow matching in the chart, and map back (Figure 4). It was built for brain connectivity: correlation matrices from fMRI and covariance matrices from EEG. The tool robotics would use for stiffness comes from neuroimaging.

4. A small experiment: which chart to generate in

I made 20,000 stiffness ellipses with largest eigenvalue between 316 and 3162 N/m, anisotropy between 2 and 50, and orientations from two task frames 60° apart. I trained the same flow-matching network (three hidden layers of 256, 6000 steps, same seed) in two charts: the raw entries \((k_{11}, k_{12}, k_{22})\), and the log chart \(\log K\), mapped back with the matrix exponential. In raw entries 4.2% of the samples were not positive definite and a further 4.7% were more anisotropic than anything in the data. In the log chart none were invalid by construction, and 1.6% fell beyond the data's anisotropy. Both reproduced the two orientation clusters (Figure 5). One seed and one dataset, so take the numbers as an illustration. The mechanism is general: the model's errors fall where the chart puts the boundary.

5. The comparison nobody has run

Papers that learn a full stiffness matrix compare against a Cholesky or Euclidean parameterisation of the same matrix, a fixed stiffness, or nothing. The closest evidence, Geiger et al. (T-RO 2026), shows that directional stiffness matters (0% success without it on non-cylindrical pegs), but their stiffness is diagonal in a task frame, set per axis from the measured wrench. That is evidence for choosing the right eigenframe, not for learning off-diagonal coupling.

6. The sensorimotor connection

  • The brain adapts the eigenframe. In Burdet et al.'s divergent field, people increased stiffness mainly along the unstable direction, rotating and stretching the ellipse rather than scaling it (review). The orientation is the learned quantity, which is the rotated-diagonal hypothesis.
  • Spring-like means symmetric. Measured postural stiffness is nearly symmetric (Mussa-Ivaldi, Hogan and Bizzi 1985), so the SPD cone is the right model class; an antisymmetric part would be a non-conservative force field.
  • Posture shapes the ellipse. Much of the variation in human endpoint stiffness comes from the arm's configuration, through \(K_x = J^{-\top}K_qJ^{-1}\). The manipulability ellipsoids that Jaquier and Calinon learn on the SPD manifold are the robot side of the same map.
  • Covariances are SPD too. Neural population covariance and connectivity matrices live on the same cone, which is why geometry-aware tools keep crossing between neuroscience and robotics.

7. What to take away

  • Generate stiffness in a chart that makes invalid outputs impossible: the log chart, or Cholesky factors with a positive diagonal.
  • Use intrinsic distances when the range is wide; a single tangent space is fine only near its base point.
  • Before learning a full matrix, test whether a diagonal in the right frame does as well.
