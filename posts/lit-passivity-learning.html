Energy Tanks and Passivity for Learned Controllers

[Literature Review]

Part of a series on robot learning, read against motor neuroscience: variable impedance · adaptation · passivity · safe RL · stiffness geometry · games. The overview is From Sensorimotor Neuroscience to Robot Learning.

Changing stiffness online can inject energy, and a learned policy changes it every step. The literature has three answers: wrap the policy in an energy tank, constrain learning to provably stable gain schedules, or build passivity into the model or controller. A simulation of 300 random stiffness schedules shows the size of the problem and the price of the fix. Without a tank a quarter of them doubled the energy and the worst grew it 17-fold. A 10 mJ tank caps every one at its passivity bound and refuses about 2% of the commanded stiffness, almost all of it while the spring is loaded.

1. Where the energy comes from

An impedance controller with a fixed target stores \(S = \tfrac12 e^\top K e\), \(e = x_d - x\). With a time-varying stiffness,

$$\dot S = -\dot x^\top K e + \tfrac12\, e^\top \dot K\, e ,$$

and the second term is supplied by no physical port: raising stiffness while the spring is stretched creates energy. Timed in phase with the motion, modest changes pump a damped oscillator exponentially (the parametric resonance derived in Passivity in One Page). Kronander and Billard (T-RO 2016) showed that a schedule of individually stable gains can be unstable and gave sufficient conditions to prevent it. Their state-independent condition asks for an \(\alpha > 0\), tied to the damping, such that \(\dot K + \alpha\dot D - 2\alpha K\) stays negative definite. Stiffness may grow, but only at a bounded rate relative to itself.

2. How bad is a learned schedule?

A trained policy's stiffness output is not adversarial, but it is not timed with the dynamics in mind either. I drove a lightly damped 1 kg spring (natural frequency 10 rad/s) with 300 random log-stiffness schedules, each an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process with a standard deviation of 0.5 (a factor of 1.6) and a correlation time between 30 ms and 1 s. Without a tank the median schedule raised the peak energy to 1.37 times its initial value, 25% of them doubled it, and the worst reached 17 times (Figure 1, left). The damage concentrates where the schedule varies near \(1/2\omega_0\), the parametric-resonance timescale.

With a tank, the worst case sits exactly on the bound \((E_0 + T_{\max})/E_0\) for every capacity (Figure 1, right). The refused stiffness ranges from 11% of the commanded log-stiffness for a 0.1 mJ tank to 2% for 10 mJ. It is not refused uniformly. A stiffness increase costs \(\tfrac12 e^\top\Delta K e\), which is negligible near equilibrium, so the tank binds only while the spring is loaded. A safety mechanism matters only where it activates. Aggregate metrics hide that, and per-state activation rates show it.

3. Tanks around a learned policy

Ferraguti, Secchi and Fantuzzi (ICRA 2013) introduced the tank for variable stiffness; Zanella et al. (IET CTA 2024) passivise an arbitrary RL policy with one, in simulation. Zhang, Solak, Hjorth and Ajoudani (RAS 2026) go further: an energy-budget constraint during training, a passivity filter at deployment, on a Franka with a force sensor. Their deployment results (Figure 2) make the point sharply. Policies trained with energy awareness still violated the budget without the filter. The filter removed the violations for every policy and, for the agnostic one, raised successes from zero to all 100 episodes. Energy awareness during training changed how fast the filter had to intervene (Figure 3). In their words, passivity cannot be guaranteed without the layer, whether or not the agent was trained with passivity awareness.

4. Stable by construction

Instead of filtering, constrain what the learner can propose. C-GMS (Kumar and Prakash, ICRA 2026) runs policy search over movement primitives and impedance schedules, but samples gains from a manifold on which the Kronander–Billard condition holds, so every rollout is stable and within actuator limits (Figure 4). When the constraint is switched off after a via-point, the same optimiser keeps reducing cost while the gains grow without bound and the motion oscillates (Figure 5).

On the learning side, structure can make the model itself passive. A port-Hamiltonian system

$$\dot x = \big(J(x) - R(x)\big)\nabla H(x) + G(x)u, \qquad y = G(x)^\top \nabla H(x),$$

with \(J = -J^\top\), \(R \succeq 0\) and \(H \ge 0\), satisfies \(\dot H = -\nabla H^\top R\, \nabla H + y^\top u \le y^\top u\) for any parameters. So a network that outputs \(H\), \(R\) and \(J\) in that form is passive however it is trained. Neary and Topcu (L4DC 2023) compose such networks through their ports. Neural contractive dynamical systems (Beik-Mohammadi et al., ICLR 2024) build contraction into a motion policy's architecture in the same spirit.

5. Learned policies that drive passive controllers

A third route keeps the learned part upstream of a controller that is passive by design. Flow with the Force Field (Li, Li, Zhang and Figueroa, ICRA 2026) generates force-informed training data from a single simulated demonstration and trains a flow-matching policy whose outputs, a velocity field and an impedance parameter, drive a passive impedance controller (Figure 6). In their block-flipping run the predicted impedance parameter drops sharply at contact (Figure 7), the stiff-to-soft switch of the model in my impedance review.

6. The sensorimotor connection

  • Coupled stability is the human situation. Colgate and Hogan (1988) proved that a linear system is stable when coupled to every passive environment if and only if it is itself passive at the port. People grasp tools, doors and other people without instability, which is the behaviour that theorem asks for.
  • Postural stiffness is spring-like. Mussa-Ivaldi, Hogan and Bizzi (1985) found the measured endpoint stiffness of the arm nearly symmetric: its curl, the part that could circulate energy around a closed path, was small. A symmetric stiffness field is conservative. It stores energy and gives it back, but it cannot pump. Learned stiffness outputs are usually constrained to be symmetric for the same reason.
  • Biology budgets energy too, metabolically. Co-contraction costs metabolic energy, and Franklin's learning rule removes it once it is no longer needed (impedance review). A tank and an effort cost are both budgets on stiffness. One is a hard constraint, the other a price.

7. Open questions

  • Which channel injects the energy? Moving the target also changes the stored energy, so a tank that guards only stiffness changes may miss the larger channel.
  • Certificates are rarely compared to tuned baselines. Stability is shown; the cost in task performance against the best fixed impedance usually is not.
  • Report where the guard binds. A tank's refusal rate per phase says whether it shaped behaviour or only sat there.
