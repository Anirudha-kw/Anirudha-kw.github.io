Rotations Form a Group: SO(3) and Its Exponential Map

[Misc]

Rotations are not vectors: you cannot add two of them, average them coordinate-wise, or step along a gradient without leaving the set. They form a Lie group, \(SO(3)\), and almost everything a learning system needs (errors, updates, interpolation, Jacobians) goes through its exponential and log maps. This note derives those maps, then follows them down to a float16 bug where the derivative of a rotation became NaN exactly where a trained policy spends its time.

1. A group that is also a manifold

\(SO(3) = \{R \in \mathbb R^{3\times 3} : R^\top R = I,\ \det R = 1\}\). Products of rotations are rotations, \(R^{-1} = R^\top\), and \(I\) is in the set, so it is a group. It is not a vector space: \(R_1 + R_2\) is generally not a rotation, and neither is \(\tfrac12(R_1 + R_2)\). It is, however, a smooth 3-dimensional surface inside the 9-dimensional space of matrices, which is what makes calculus possible.

2. The tangent space at the identity

Take any smooth curve of rotations with \(R(0) = I\) and differentiate the constraint \(R(t)^\top R(t) = I\) at \(t = 0\):

$$\dot R(0)^\top + \dot R(0) = 0 .$$

So the tangent vectors at the identity are exactly the skew-symmetric matrices. They form the Lie algebra \(\mathfrak{so}(3)\), three-dimensional, and the hat map identifies them with vectors in \(\mathbb R^3\):

$$\hat\omega = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -\omega_3 & \omega_2 \\ \omega_3 & 0 & -\omega_1 \\ -\omega_2 & \omega_1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \hat\omega\, x = \omega \times x .$$

Tangent vectors at another rotation \(R\) are \(R\hat\omega\): translate the algebra by the group. Because rotations preserve cross products, \(R(\omega\times x) = (R\omega)\times(Rx)\), the hat map is equivariant,

$$R\,\hat\omega\,R^\top = \widehat{R\omega},$$

which is the identity behind every "rotate the angular velocity into the world frame" step.

3. The exponential map, derived

A constant angular velocity \(\omega\) for unit time solves \(\dot R = R\hat\omega\), so it ends at \(\exp(\hat\omega) = \sum_k \hat\omega^k / k!\). Write \(\omega = \theta u\) with \(\|u\| = 1\). The triple cross product \(u\times(u\times x) = u(u^\top x) - x\) says \(\hat u^2 = uu^\top - I\), hence

$$\hat u^3 = \hat u\,(uu^\top - I) = -\hat u .$$

Every power of \(\hat u\) is therefore \(\pm\hat u\) or \(\pm\hat u^2\), and the series splits into the sine and cosine series:

$$\exp(\theta\hat u) = I + \Big(\theta - \frac{\theta^3}{3!} + \cdots\Big)\hat u + \Big(\frac{\theta^2}{2!} - \frac{\theta^4}{4!} + \cdots\Big)\hat u^2 = I + \sin\theta\,\hat u + (1-\cos\theta)\,\hat u^2 .$$

This is Rodrigues' formula. In terms of \(\omega\) it reads \(I + \frac{\sin\theta}{\theta}\hat\omega + \frac{1-\cos\theta}{\theta^2}\hat\omega^2\). Applied to a vector it has the geometry in Figure 1.

4. The log map

Taking the trace of Rodrigues' formula, with \(\operatorname{tr}\hat u = 0\) and \(\operatorname{tr}\hat u^2 = \operatorname{tr}(uu^\top - I) = -2\), gives \(\operatorname{tr}R = 1 + 2\cos\theta\). The skew part isolates the axis, since \(\hat u^2\) is symmetric: \(R - R^\top = 2\sin\theta\,\hat u\). So for \(\theta\in(0,\pi)\)

$$\theta = \arccos\frac{\operatorname{tr}R - 1}{2}, \qquad \hat\omega = \log R = \frac{\theta}{2\sin\theta}\,(R - R^\top).$$

At \(\theta = \pi\) the skew part vanishes and the axis must be read off the symmetric part instead: one of the singularities every three-parameter chart of \(SO(3)\) must have somewhere.

5. Errors, updates and Jacobians

Learning on rotations uses these maps in three places. The error between \(R\) and a target \(R_d\) is the 3-vector \(\log(R_d^\top R)^\vee\). An update with gradient \(g\) is \(R \leftarrow R\exp(-\eta\hat g)\), which stays on the group for any step size. And the derivative of \(\exp\) is the right Jacobian,

$$J_r(\omega) = I - \frac{1-\cos\theta}{\theta^2}\,\hat\omega + \frac{\theta - \sin\theta}{\theta^3}\,\hat\omega^2, \qquad \exp\big(\widehat{\omega + \delta}\big) \approx \exp(\hat\omega)\exp\big(\widehat{J_r(\omega)\delta}\big).$$

Every coefficient here is a removable \(0/0\) at the identity: \(\frac{1-\cos\theta}{\theta^2} = \frac12 - \frac{\theta^2}{24} + \cdots\) and \(\frac{\theta-\sin\theta}{\theta^3} = \frac16 - \frac{\theta^2}{120} + \cdots\).

6. Where the numbers go wrong

Evaluated literally, \(1 - \cos\theta\) subtracts two numbers that agree in every bit once \(\theta\) is small, and the coefficient silently becomes zero: below 0.022 rad in float16 and below \(2.4\times10^{-4}\) rad in float32. The rewrite \(1-\cos\theta = 2\sin^2(\theta/2)\) has no subtraction and stays accurate (Figure 2). The same care applies to recovering the angle from a quaternion \(q = (w, v)\): \(\theta = 2\arccos w\) has an infinite derivative at \(w = 1\), while \(\theta = 2\,\operatorname{atan2}(\|v\|, w)\) is well-conditioned everywhere.

7. A gradient that turned into NaN

Quaternions double-cover \(SO(3)\) (\(q\) and \(-q\) are the same rotation) and avoid the singularity of three-parameter charts, so they are the usual state; axis–angle \(\omega = \theta\,v/\ell\) with \(\ell = \|v\|\) is the usual action or error. Its Jacobian with respect to \(v\) is

$$\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial v} = \theta'(\ell)\,uu^\top + \frac{\theta}{\ell}\,\big(I - uu^\top\big), \qquad u = \frac{v}{\ell},\quad \theta = 2\operatorname{atan2}(\ell, w),\quad \theta'(\ell) = \frac{2w}{w^2+\ell^2}.$$

Every term is bounded near the identity: \(\theta/\ell\to 2/w\) and \(\theta'\to 2/w\), so the Jacobian tends to \(2I\). Differentiate \(v/\ell\) term by term instead and you get \(I/\ell - vv^\top/\ell^3\). The two pieces cancel analytically, but numerically \(1/\ell^3\) overflows first: float16's largest number is 65504, so \(\ell^{-3}\) is infinite once \(\ell < 0.025\), and \(0\cdot\infty\) in the off-diagonal entries makes the whole Jacobian NaN. In NVIDIA Warp's adjoint of quat_to_axis_angle this happened for every rotation below 0.047 rad in float16, and below \(3\times10^{-13}\) rad in float32 (Figure 3). Writing the Jacobian through the unit axis, as above, is algebraically identical and finite (issue, fix).

8. What to take away

  • Represent errors and updates in the Lie algebra, and move on the group with \(\exp\). Never add matrices or quaternion components.
  • Every closed-form coefficient on \(SO(3)\) has a removable singularity at the identity, and the identity is where converged policies live. Test small angles in the lowest precision you train with.
  • Analytic cancellation is not numerical cancellation. If two terms cancel in the algebra, cancel them before the code runs.
