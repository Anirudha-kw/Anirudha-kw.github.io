Neural Population Dynamics and Policies as Dynamical Systems

[Literature Review]

Part of a series on sensorimotor neuroscience and robot learning: impedance · internal models · optimal feedback control · touch and grip · synergies and primitives · population dynamics.

For decades, neurons in motor cortex were described by what they "encode": direction, speed, force. A newer view treats the population as a dynamical system whose state evolves by its own rules, with preparation setting the initial condition and movement unfolding as the dynamics play out. That is also the natural way to describe a robot policy, and it suggests what to look for inside one.

1. From tuning to dynamics

Shenoy, Sahani and Churchland (2013) lay out the dynamical-systems perspective. Instead of asking which movement parameter each neuron represents, model the population activity \(x(t)\) as

$$\dot x = f(x) + u(t),$$

where \(u\) is input from other areas. Preparatory activity before a movement drives the state to a condition-specific initial value; once movement begins, the intrinsic dynamics \(f\) generate the time-varying pattern that drives the muscles. Under this view much of single-neuron "tuning" is a side effect of the dynamics, not a code.

2. Rotational structure during reaching

Churchland et al. (2012) recorded populations in motor and premotor cortex of monkeys during reaching and asked whether the population state rotates. Their method, jPCA, fits linear dynamics \(\dot x = Mx\) to the population trajectories with \(M\) constrained to be skew-symmetric, \(M^\top = -M\), so that the fitted flow is pure rotation, and then projects onto the plane of the fastest rotation. In that plane the activity for different reach conditions rotated in the same direction at a similar frequency, starting from different initial states set during preparation: a quasi-oscillatory structure that tuning models had not predicted.

Figure 1 shows why a dedicated method was needed, on simulated data. When condition-independent signals and static condition offsets carry most of the variance, the top principal components show a tangle; the skew-symmetric fit picks out the rotation underneath.

3. Networks that find the same solution

Sussillo et al. (2015) trained recurrent neural networks to produce recorded muscle activity from simple inputs. Networks regularised toward simple, smooth dynamics developed population activity that resembled motor cortex, including its rotational structure. The resemblance depended on the regularisation, which is the interesting part: biology's solution looks like the one a network finds when it is pushed toward simple dynamics.

4. Policies as dynamical systems

Robotics reached a similar view from control. Dynamic movement primitives (see synergies and primitives) and stable estimators of dynamical systems such as SEDS (Khansari-Zadeh and Billard, T-RO 2011), which learns a nonlinear dynamical system from demonstrations with guaranteed convergence to the target, describe a skill as a flow field rather than a trajectory. A recurrent policy is a learned dynamical system too, and its internal state can be analysed with the same tools.

5. What to do with this as a roboticist

  • Preparation as initial condition. Many learned policies receive the goal at every step. The cortical picture suggests an alternative: set an internal state from the goal, then let dynamics run. That separates what to do from how to unfold it.
  • Analyse policies like populations. Fitting linear or skew-symmetric dynamics to a trained policy's hidden states is cheap and says whether it has learned an oscillator, an integrator or a lookup table.
  • Regularise toward simple dynamics. Sussillo's result suggests that pressure toward smooth, low-complexity dynamics produces more brain-like and plausibly more robust solutions; for robot policies it is a testable hypothesis about generalisation.
