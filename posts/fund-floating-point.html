Floating Point for Robot Learning

[Fundamentals]

Fundamentals: optimisation · neural networks · floating point · memory and the 1 kHz loop · GPUs · processes and the kernel · dynamics algorithms · deploying policies · policy files · how neurons compute · bits, delays and control. Measured on my workstation (Intel i7-9700, RTX 2070 SUPER, Ubuntu 24.04, Linux 6.8); code in the site's tools/posts/.

A computer stores a real number as a sign, an exponent and a fixed number of significant bits, so every format trades range against precision. Robot learning now mixes fp64 simulators, fp32 GPU simulators and fp16 or bf16 networks, and each boundary is a place where numbers silently change. Four measurements below: how coarse each format is, how sums go wrong, why half-precision training needs loss scaling, and how precision and summation order make a simulation diverge.

1. The formats

A normal floating-point number is \((-1)^s \times 1.f \times 2^{e - \text{bias}}\). The fraction width sets the relative precision \(\varepsilon\), the gap between 1 and the next number. The exponent width sets the range. Between consecutive powers of two the numbers are evenly spaced, so the absolute gap grows with magnitude (Figure 1).

The consequence for robotics is concrete. A position 1 km from the world origin in fp32 can only move in steps of 61 µm. GPU simulators run in fp32, so a robot placed far from the origin, or a long trajectory integrated in world coordinates, loses contact resolution. Keep scenes near the origin and express contact quantities in local frames. A quaternion integrated in fp32 drifts off unit norm and needs renormalising.

2. Sums go wrong in a predictable way

Each addition rounds with relative error up to \(\varepsilon/2\). Adding \(n\) positive numbers one at a time accumulates error that grows roughly like \(n\varepsilon\) in the worst case. Pairwise (tree) summation, which NumPy's sum uses, grows like \(\varepsilon\log n\). Kahan's compensated summation (1965) carries the lost low bits forward and stays near \(\varepsilon\) (Higham 1993). Figure 2 measures all three. At \(n = 10^6\) the sequential fp32 sum is off by 5.6e-06 relative, the pairwise sum by 6.5e-08. A sequential fp16 sum stops growing entirely: once the running total reaches 2048, the gap between fp16 numbers is 2 and adding anything below 1 rounds to nothing. Its result stays near 2048 while the true sum is about 500,000, an error of 99.6%.

Cancellation is the other classic. Computing \(1 - \cos\theta\) for small \(\theta\) subtracts two nearly equal numbers and keeps only rounding noise; \(2\sin^2(\theta/2)\) is the same quantity computed stably. The float16 rotation gradient that turned into NaN in my SO(3) note is this failure.

3. Why half-precision training needs loss scaling

fp16 keeps 11 significant bits but its smallest normal number is \(6.1\times10^{-5}\). Below that come subnormals, which lose precision, and below \(2^{-25}\) values flush to zero. Gradients are small, especially late in training. I trained a policy-sized MLP (32-256-256-7) to a loss of 2.4e-05 and collected every parameter gradient (Figure 3). 98% of them sat in fp16's subnormal range and 1.2% would have become zero. Multiplying the loss by \(2^{12}\) before the backward pass, and dividing the gradients after, moves all but 0.6% into the normal range and flushes none (Micikevicius et al. 2018). bf16 avoids the problem with fp32's exponent range, at the cost of keeping fewer than three significant decimal digits (Kalamkar et al. 2019).

4. Precision and order in simulation

Two runs of the same integrator in fp32 and fp64 start identical and separate. For a 2 Hz spring stepped at 1 kHz with symplectic Euler, the positions differ by 5.9e-07 after 1 s, 4.8e-06 after 10 s and 8.2e-05 after 200 s, relative to an amplitude of 1 (Figure 4, left). For chaotic systems, such as a block tumbling on contacts, the separation grows exponentially instead, so fp32 and fp64 simulators produce different rollouts from the same initial state.

Floating-point addition is also not associative. Summing the same \(10^5\) contact-force-sized numbers in 400 random orders in fp32 gave 304 distinct results, spread over 0.077 on a total of 908 (Figure 4, right). GPU reductions with atomic adds sum in whatever order the threads arrive. That is why GPU simulators and training runs are not bit-reproducible unless a deterministic mode fixes the order.

5. A note on brains

Neurons are noisy, low-precision devices, and the nervous system gets precision by averaging over populations and time (see the population-coding and noise discussion in the overview). Low-precision arithmetic has an analogue in stochastic rounding: round up or down at random with probabilities that make the result unbiased. Gupta et al. (2015) trained networks in 16-bit fixed point with it, where round to nearest failed. Noise that averages out is cheaper than precision.

6. Checklist

  • Keep simulated scenes near the origin; work in local frames for contact.
  • Accumulate long sums (returns, energies, statistics) in fp64 or with pairwise or compensated summation.
  • In fp16 training, use dynamic loss scaling; on hardware without bf16 tensor cores, fp16 is the fast half format (see the GPU note).
  • Expect fp32 and fp64 simulators to diverge on chaotic contact tasks, and GPU runs to be non-deterministic unless you ask otherwise.
