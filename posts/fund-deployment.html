Running a Policy on Real Hardware

[Fundamentals]

Fundamentals: optimisation · neural networks · floating point · memory and the 1 kHz loop · GPUs · processes and the kernel · dynamics algorithms · deploying policies · policy files · how neurons compute · bits, delays and control. Measured on my workstation (Intel i7-9700, RTX 2070 SUPER, Ubuntu 24.04, Linux 6.8); code in the site's tools/posts/.

A policy that will run on a robot meets a different cost model from the one it was trained under. At batch size one, the arithmetic of a small network takes a few microseconds; the rest is overhead from the framework, the language, the operating system and the bus. I ran one 64-256-256-7 policy through every CPU path: 54 µs in eager PyTorch, 27 µs as TorchScript, 15 µs in NumPy, and 6.7 µs in plain C, but only after letting the compiler reorder floating-point sums. In strict order the C version took 56 µs. Generative policies multiply the cost by their number of denoising steps. Action chunking hides that latency at the price of open-loop execution.

1. Where the time goes at batch size one

The network does about 84,000 multiply-adds per call. A modern core can do tens of billions per second, so the floor is a few microseconds. Figure 1 shows how far each path is from it, on one pinned core.

  • Eager PyTorch (54 µs) dispatches each operator through Python and the autograd machinery. inference_mode drops the autograd bookkeeping (38 µs). TorchScript, traced and frozen, removes Python between operators (27 µs).
  • torch.compile generated C++ for the graph but was slower here (65 µs): at this size its guard checks and call overhead outweigh what it fuses. Compilers pay off on larger graphs and batches.
  • Dynamic int8 quantisation was the slowest (108 µs), with a maximum output error of 0.0024. Quantising the activations and dequantising the results on every call costs more than the int8 arithmetic saves for three small layers. Quantisation helps when weights dominate memory traffic or many inputs are batched (Jacob et al. 2018).
  • NumPy (15 µs) is three small matrix-vector products with almost no dispatch overhead.
  • Plain C took 56 µs compiled with -O3 -march=native, and 6.7 µs once I added -fassociative-math. Each output is a long sum of products. IEEE arithmetic is not associative, so without permission to reorder, the compiler must add the terms one at a time, in order. With permission it keeps eight partial sums in an AVX2 register and uses fused multiply-adds. The outputs agreed to 4×10−07 either way. The floating-point note shows why reordering changes results in general; for one dot product, it changes the speed eightfold.

2. On the GPU

For a larger, ACT-sized transformer policy (four layers, width 256, 32 tokens, 3.2M parameters), the GPU's problem at batch size one is launch overhead, as the GPU note measured. Eager fp32 takes 911 µs per call and eager fp16 899 µs, nearly all of it spent launching the forward pass's many kernels. Captured in a CUDA graph the same network takes 304 µs in fp32 and 177 µs in fp16; torch.compile in reduce-overhead mode, which also uses CUDA graphs, takes 267 µs (Figure 2, left). fp16 changed the outputs by at most 0.001.

3. Generative policies pay per denoising step

Diffusion and flow-matching policies generate an action chunk by integrating a learned vector field over \(K\) steps (Chi et al. 2023; flow matching), so latency is \(K\) times one network evaluation. For a 0.68M-parameter denoiser producing a 16-step, 7-D chunk, one step costs 117 µs on a CPU core. A 100-step DDPM sampler then takes 11.7 ms and fits a 10 Hz loop but not a 100 Hz one; 10 steps (DDIM, Song et al. 2021, or a flow) take 1.2 ms; one step fits inside a 1 kHz period. On the GPU, with the whole sampling loop captured in one CUDA graph, the same counts take 2.8, 0.32 and 0.04 ms (Figure 2, right), so even 100 steps fit a 100 Hz loop, and a one-step consistency policy (Prasad et al. 2024) fits 1 kHz with room to spare.

4. Chunking hides latency and opens the loop

Diffusion Policy and ACT (Zhao et al. 2023) predict chunks of future actions and execute several before asking again. That lets a slow policy keep up with a fast robot. Inference for the next chunk runs while the current one executes, and a chunk needs to be at least as long as the inference latency. But every executed action was computed from an observation that is older by the latency plus its position in the chunk. Figure 3 simulates this on a target that moves unpredictably, with a policy that extrapolates the target's last observed velocity. With 20 ms inference, the tracking error is 3 mm at the shortest chunk and 149 mm at a 600 ms chunk. At 300 ms inference no chunk is short enough to track well.

Two remedies are in use. ACT averages overlapping chunks (temporal ensembling). Real-time chunking (Black, Galliker and Levine, NeurIPS 2025) generates the next chunk while the current one executes, treating the actions already committed as fixed, so the boundary is continuous and inference never stalls the robot.

5. An architecture that respects the clocks

  • A 1 kHz impedance or torque loop in compiled code, close to the hardware (processes, the 1 kHz loop).
  • The learned policy at 10–50 Hz in its own process, sending targets and gains (stiffness, damping) through shared memory. That is the variable-impedance action space of my impedance review.
  • Interpolate between the policy's targets in the fast loop; never let a late policy step stall it.

6. The sensorimotor connection

People also act on stale information, and the nervous system hides that latency much as chunking does. The basal ganglia are implicated in grouping practised actions into chunks that run as units (Graybiel 1998), and visually guided corrections arrive 100 ms or more after the event. Forward models predict the present from delayed signals, and fast reflex loops handle what the plan did not foresee. A chunked policy without the reflex layer is like a person moving with their eyes closed.

7. Checklist

  • Measure batch-1 latency on the deployment machine, by path; framework overhead dominates small networks.
  • For tiny policies, a hand-written or NumPy forward pass beats the framework. Allow reassociation if you write C.
  • On the GPU, capture the forward pass, or the whole sampling loop, in a CUDA graph.
  • Count denoising steps against the control period. Chunk only as long as the latency requires, and keep a fast reactive loop underneath.
