Passivity in One Page

[Misc]

Passivity is the property that makes physical interaction safe to compose: a passive robot touching a passive environment cannot go unstable, whatever the environment is. This note derives it, proves the interconnection theorem in three lines, and then shows how a learned controller breaks it. Modulating stiffness at the right frequency pumps energy into a damped spring exponentially, a parametric resonance, and an energy tank caps it at what was there to begin with.

1. Storage, supply and passivity

Take a system with input \(u\), output \(y\) and state \(x\). It is passive if there is a storage function \(S(x) \ge 0\) such that, along every trajectory,

$$\dot S \le y^\top u \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad S\big(x(t)\big) - S\big(x(0)\big) \le \int_0^t y^\top u\, d\tau .$$

The product \(y^\top u\) is the power supplied through the port; at a mechanical contact it is force times velocity. Passivity says the system can never deliver more energy than was stored in it initially plus what was put in. Springs, masses and dampers are passive, and so is almost any environment a robot touches.

2. Why it composes: the interconnection theorem

Connect two passive systems in negative feedback with external inputs \(r_1, r_2\): \(u_1 = r_1 - y_2\), \(u_2 = r_2 + y_1\). With \(S = S_1 + S_2\),

$$\dot S \le y_1^\top u_1 + y_2^\top u_2 = y_1^\top(r_1 - y_2) + y_2^\top(r_2 + y_1) = y_1^\top r_1 + y_2^\top r_2 .$$

The cross terms cancel, so the closed loop is passive from \((r_1, r_2)\) to \((y_1, y_2)\). With no external input, \(\dot S \le 0\): if \(S\) is positive definite it is a Lyapunov function and the interconnection is stable. This is the whole argument for passivity-based interaction control: make the robot plus controller passive at the contact port, and stability holds for every passive environment, including ones you never modelled.

3. A constant impedance is passive

The impedance law \(F = K(x_d - x) - D\dot x\) with constant SPD \(K\), \(D \succeq 0\) and a fixed target has storage \(S = \tfrac12 e^\top K e\), \(e = x_d - x\). Since \(\dot e = -\dot x\),

$$\dot S = e^\top K\dot e = -\dot x^\top K e, \qquad \dot x^\top F = \dot x^\top K e - \dot x^\top D\dot x = -\dot S - \dot x^\top D \dot x ,$$

so the energy the controller delivers through the port is exactly the decrease of its spring energy minus what the damper burns. It cannot exceed what was stored.

4. Changing stiffness breaks it, resonantly

With \(K(t)\) the storage gains a term that no port supplies,

$$\dot S = -\dot x^\top K e + \tfrac12\, e^\top \dot K\, e ,$$

positive whenever stiffness rises while the spring is loaded. A learned policy changes stiffness every step, and nothing stops it from doing so in phase with the motion. The worst case is classical: a damped oscillator whose stiffness is modulated at twice its natural frequency,

$$\ddot x + 2\lambda\dot x + \omega_0^2\big(1 + \varepsilon\sin 2\omega_0 t\big)\,x = 0,$$

is unstable when \(\varepsilon\omega_0/4 > \lambda\), with energy growing at the rate \(2(\varepsilon\omega_0/4 - \lambda)\) (the parametric-resonance condition; see Landau and Lifshitz, Mechanics). For \(\omega_0 = 10\) rad/s, \(\lambda = 0.2\) s\(^{-1}\) and \(\varepsilon = 0.3\) the prediction is 1.10 s\(^{-1}\); the simulation in Figure 1 grows at 1.10 s\(^{-1}\), and the mechanical energy ends 6,486 times larger than it started, despite the damper. No single instant looks dangerous; a ±30% stiffness change is modest. The timing is what does it.

5. Energy tanks

The fix (Ferraguti, Secchi and Fantuzzi, ICRA 2013) is bookkeeping. Add a virtual energy store \(T \ge 0\), the tank. Energy the damper dissipates, and energy released when stiffness decreases under load, flows into the tank. A stiffness increase \(\Delta K\) at displacement \(e\) costs \(\tfrac12 e^\top \Delta K\, e\) and is allowed only if the tank can pay; otherwise it is scaled down to what the tank holds. With the augmented storage \(S + T\), every term that used to appear from nowhere is now a transfer out of \(T\), so

$$\frac{d}{dt}(S + T) \le y^\top u ,$$

and the closed loop is passive again, for any stiffness schedule the policy asks for. An upper bound on \(T\) keeps the tank from saving up enough energy to do damage later.

In the simulation the tank starts with 0.02 J and is refilled by the damper, so the bound is concrete: the mechanical energy can never exceed the initial 0.125 J plus the tank's 0.02 J, 1.16 times the start. It ends at 1.15. The tank does not make the system decay, because energy the damper burns is recycled into stiffening; it makes it unable to run away. 6. What the tank does not cover

  • Moving the target. A jump in \(x_d\) changes the stored energy instantly and is a second non-passive channel, and a stiffness-only tank does not guard it.
  • Performance. When the tank is empty, stiffness freezes. A policy that relies on stiffening at the wrong moment loses that ability exactly when it is unsafe, which is correct and also a change to its behaviour that training should see.
  • Discretisation. A sampled controller with a zero-order hold injects energy of its own at high gains. Tanks bound the controller's bookkeeping, not the gap between the continuous model and the sampled one.

7. What to take away

  • Passivity is what lets a controller be safe in environments it has never seen; it composes, stability proofs do not.
  • Variable stiffness is not passive by itself, and the danger is in the timing of the changes, not their size.
  • Account for every channel through which energy can enter, then gate it. See Energy Tanks and Passivity for Learned Controllers for how the literature applies this to learning.
