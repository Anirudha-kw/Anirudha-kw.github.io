Bits, Delays and Control

[Fundamentals]

Fundamentals: optimisation · neural networks · floating point · memory and the 1 kHz loop · GPUs · processes and the kernel · dynamics algorithms · deploying policies · policy files · how neurons compute · bits, delays and control. Measured on my workstation (Intel i7-9700, RTX 2070 SUPER, Ubuntu 24.04, Linux 6.8); code in the site's tools/posts/.

Control is information processing under a deadline. Three classical results connect human movement, robot control and communication, and each is computed here. First, signal-dependent noise makes movement time depend only on the ratio of distance to target width, the scale invariance behind Fitts' law; in my model the curve is a power law rather than a logarithm. Second, stabilising an unstable system needs more than \(\log_2|a|\) bits per measurement: the simulation fails exactly below that threshold. Third, delay caps feedback gain; a predictor built from an internal model lifts the cap, which is one account of what the cerebellum does.

1. Speed, accuracy and information

Fitts (1954) found that the time to move a distance \(D\) to a target of width \(W\) grows with the index of difficulty, \(MT = a + b\log_2(D/W + 1)\) in the Shannon form (MacKenzie 1992). He read \(1/b\) as the information capacity of the motor system, in bits per second. Why should distance and width enter only as a ratio? Signal-dependent noise (Harris and Wolpert 1998; overview) gives one answer. If the noise in each command is proportional to its size, scaling a movement up scales the endpoint spread by the same factor, so only \(D/W\) matters.

Figure 1 tests that with the minimum-variance reaching model from the overview: for each \(D\) and \(W\), I found the shortest movement whose endpoint spread fits the target (96% within \(W\)). The points for different distances collapse onto one curve of \(D/W\), as predicted. But the curve is not Fitts' logarithm. A power law with exponent 0.92 fits with \(R^2 = 0.93\), against \(0.77\) for the logarithm. Meyer et al. (1988) derived a power law (a square root) from optimally planned submovements. Real data cover a narrow enough range that both forms fit; what the model gets robustly is the dependence on the ratio.

Robots break this invariance. Their positioning noise comes mostly from encoders, sensing and backlash, which do not grow with the commanded distance, and their movement time is set by torque limits: about \(2\sqrt{D/a_{\max}}\) for a bang-bang move. A robot's speed–accuracy trade-off is a question about bandwidth and resolution, not about \(D/W\).

2. How many bits it takes to stay upright

Suppose the controller of an unstable system \(x_{t+1} = a x_t + u_t + w_t\) receives its measurement through a channel of \(R\) bits per step. Each step the uncertainty about \(x\) grows by a factor \(|a|\), and \(R\) bits can shrink it by at most \(2^R\). The data-rate theorem (Nair and Evans 2004; Tatikonda and Mitter 2004) makes this exact: the system can be stabilised if and only if \(R > \log_2|a|\), summed over unstable modes. Figure 2 implements the standard construction. Coder and decoder share a shrinking interval, the measurement is quantised to \(2^R\) cells, and the control cancels the estimate. Every run stays bounded once \(R\) exceeds \(\log_2 a\), and every run is lost below it.

In continuous time the bound is \(\sum \mathrm{Re}\,\lambda_i/\ln 2\) bits per second. An inverted pendulum 1 m long has an unstable pole at \(\sqrt{g/l} \approx 3.1\) s\(^{-1}\), so balancing it needs at least 4.5 bits per second of information about its state. The number is small; the hard part is getting those bits on time. Quantised encoders, network-limited teleoperation and low-rate policies are all channels in this sense.

3. Delay limits gain; prediction removes the limit

Feed back the position of an integrating plant (\(\dot x = u\)) through a delay \(\tau\) with gain \(K\). The loop gains phase \(-\omega\tau\) from the delay, and it goes unstable when \(K > \pi/(2\tau)\). With a 100 ms delay, roughly a visual correction, that caps \(K\) near 16 s\(^{-1}\) (Figure 3, right). Even at gain 14 the response rings for a second (Figure 3, left). A Smith predictor, a classic process-control design, adds the output of an internal model to the delayed measurement, predicting where the plant is now. The loop then behaves as if there were no delay, and the same gain gives a clean response. With a model 30% wrong the prediction degrades, but the loop stays usable.

Miall, Weir, Wolpert and Stein (1993) proposed that the cerebellum is such a predictor: a forward model that lets the motor system act on its estimate of the present rather than on stale feedback. Robots need the same thing whenever a learned policy sees the world late, through a camera pipeline, a network or a slow inference step (deploying policies). Sampling adds delay too. A zero-order hold behaves roughly like half a sample of delay, which is why control loops run 10 to 20 times faster than the bandwidth they need (Åström and Murray).

4. What to take away

  • Noise that grows with the command makes accuracy scale with distance; robots usually lack that, and their trade-offs live in bandwidth and resolution.
  • An unstable system needs a minimum information rate, in bits per second, set by its unstable poles.
  • Delay caps the useful feedback gain at about \(\pi/2\tau\); an internal model that predicts the present removes the cap, as long as the model is good.
