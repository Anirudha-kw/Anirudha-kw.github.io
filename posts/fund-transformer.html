The Transformer, From 2017 to Now

[Fundamentals]

Language models today are not quite the Transformer of 2017. Most encode position differently, use a gated feed-forward layer, and let attention heads share their keys and values. I added these changes one at a time to a small language model trained on Shakespeare and measured what each one buys. Rotary position embeddings gave the largest gain, a gated feed-forward layer a smaller one, and shared keys and values cut the memory used while generating by four for almost nothing. Moving the normalisation, as most modern models do, made this shallow model worse. The best model needs 6.4% fewer bits per byte of text than the 2017 one. None of these changes is mine; the point is to see what each is worth under one setup.

Setup

A byte-level language model: it reads Shakespeare’s complete works (Project Gutenberg #100; 4.9 MB to train on, 0.5 MB held out) one byte at a time and predicts the next. Four layers, width 128, four heads, a context of 128 bytes, tied input and output embeddings: about 0.8M parameters, small enough to train on a CPU. Every model trains for 1500 steps of 32 × 128 bytes with AdamW at a learning rate of 2 × 10−3 (the 2017 model did worse at 10−3), with two seeds, and for a given seed every model sees the same batches. Each variant is checked against a reference before it is trusted: fused attention against attention written out, and generation with a cache against recomputing the whole sequence, which agree to 4 × 10−6.

Rotary position embeddings. The 2017 model adds a fixed vector to each byte to mark where it is. RoPE (Su et al.) instead rotates every query and key by an angle proportional to its position, so the score between two bytes depends only on how far apart they are. It was the largest single gain, 0.08 nats.

SwiGLU. The feed-forward layer becomes gated: one projection decides, element by element, how much of another passes through (Shazeer). With the hidden width cut to 8/3 of the model width, so the parameter count stays the same, it gained another 0.02 nats.

Grouped-query attention. All four query heads share one key and one value head (Ainslie et al.): 11% fewer parameters and a four times smaller cache of keys and values when generating, for 0.008 nats.

Pre-norm and RMSNorm. Most modern models normalise each sublayer’s input instead of the sum after it, and drop the mean from the norm. At four layers both made the model worse. At sixteen layers, with the same number of parameters, pre-norm caught up (1.530 against 1.533 nats, one seed). This matches Liu et al.: post-norm can end lower when it trains at all, and what pre-norm buys is that very deep stacks train reliably. So the figure keeps Add & Norm where the paper put it.

Not just per step. Trained a third longer, which took more wall time than the best model’s 1500 steps, the 2017 model reached 1.564, still well behind.

Speed

Measured on a CPU, one threadResult
Fused attention against attention written out (forward and backward, 2 sequences × 8 heads)1.6× faster at 128 bytes, 1.8× at 512, 3.2× at 1024, 5.4× at 2048, and never stores the 2048 × 2048 attention matrix (256 MB here)
Generating 256 bytes with a key-value cache against recomputing the sequence7.7–9.2× faster
Key-value cache per generated byte4 KB with four key-value heads, 1 KB with one

Fused attention (FlashAttention, Dao et al.) and the cache change how the model is run, not the model: the 2017 model can use both. And on a CPU, without kernels fused together, RoPE and SwiGLU made each training step up to 32% slower. I have not measured a GPU.

Limits

Small models, one dataset, 1500 steps and two seeds. A gain at this scale need not carry over to large models, in size or even in sign, and pre-norm is the example: it hurt here and is standard there for good reason.
