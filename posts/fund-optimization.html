Optimization for Robot Learning, From First Principles

[Fundamentals]

Fundamentals: optimisation · neural networks · floating point · memory and the 1 kHz loop · GPUs · processes and the kernel · dynamics algorithms · deploying policies · policy files · how neurons compute · bits, delays and control. Measured on my workstation (Intel i7-9700, RTX 2070 SUPER, Ubuntu 24.04, Linux 6.8); code in the site's tools/posts/.

Almost everything in robot learning is an optimiser run: fitting a dynamics model, training a policy, tuning gains. This note builds the main ideas from one model, a quadratic bowl, and measures each claim. Gradient descent needs iterations in proportion to the condition number, momentum in proportion to its square root, Adam fixes ill-conditioning along the axes but not across them, and the natural gradient makes a policy update independent of how the policy is parametrised. It ends with learning rates that adapt to how consistent the errors are, which is also what people do.

1. One model explains most of it

Near a minimum, any smooth loss looks like a quadratic, \(f(x) \approx f^\ast + \tfrac12 (x - x^\ast)^\top H (x - x^\ast)\), with Hessian eigenvalues between \(\mu\) and \(L\). Gradient descent \(x \leftarrow x - \eta\nabla f\) multiplies the error along eigenvector \(i\) by \(1 - \eta\lambda_i\). It is stable only if \(\eta < 2/L\). The best fixed step, \(\eta = 2/(L+\mu)\), contracts every direction by at least

$$\rho_{\text{GD}} = \frac{\kappa - 1}{\kappa + 1},\qquad \kappa = \frac{L}{\mu}, \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad k \approx \frac{\kappa}{2}\ln\frac{1}{\epsilon}\ \text{iterations}.$$

The step is limited by the steepest direction and progress by the flattest. The condition number is the whole story, and it is set by your choices: unnormalised observations, joint angles next to forces in newtons, a stiffness in N/m next to a damping ratio.

2. Momentum turns \(\kappa\) into \(\sqrt\kappa\)

Polyak's heavy ball adds a fraction of the previous step, \(x_{k+1} = x_k - \eta\nabla f(x_k) + \beta(x_k - x_{k-1})\). With \(\eta = 4/(\sqrt L + \sqrt\mu)^2\) and \(\beta = \big((\sqrt\kappa - 1)/(\sqrt\kappa + 1)\big)^2\), each eigendirection becomes a damped oscillator, and all of them decay at \((\sqrt\kappa-1)/(\sqrt\kappa+1)\). Nesterov's variant evaluates the gradient at the look-ahead point and reaches the same order, which is optimal for first-order methods on smooth convex problems. Figure 1 measures it on a 50-dimensional quadratic: at \(\kappa = 10^4\), gradient descent needs 61,106 iterations to reduce the error a millionfold, heavy ball 990 and Nesterov 1,271.

3. What Adam fixes, and what it does not

Adam keeps running averages of the gradient \(m\) and of its square \(v\), and steps \(-\alpha\,\hat m/(\sqrt{\hat v} + \epsilon)\). Dividing by \(\sqrt{\hat v}\) rescales each coordinate separately, so Adam is invariant to stretching the axes: a loss that is steep in \(x_1\) and flat in \(x_2\) looks round to it. It is not invariant to rotation. Ill-conditioning that couples coordinates survives a diagonal rescaling. Figure 2 shows both cases. With the ill-conditioning along the axes, Adam (cosine-decayed step, 2000 steps) reaches \(10^{-12}\) at every condition number. With the same matrix rotated by 35°, it stalls near \(10^{-5}\) once \(\kappa \ge 100\). For moderate \(\kappa\) plain gradient descent does better; Adam wins again only when \(\kappa\) is so large that gradient descent barely moves.

For robot learning this is concrete. Normalising each observation and action dimension is the part Adam can absorb anyway. Coupling it cannot absorb: two joints whose effects on the end effector nearly cancel, or a full stiffness matrix parametrised by its entries. Those need a reparametrisation, such as task-space coordinates, Cholesky or log factors, or a second-order method.

4. Policy gradients and the natural gradient

For a stochastic policy \(\pi_\theta(a\mid s)\), the score-function identity gives an unbiased gradient from samples (Williams 1992),

$$\nabla_\theta J = \mathbb E\big[\nabla_\theta \log\pi_\theta(a\mid s)\, A(s,a)\big],$$

with an advantage \(A\) that subtracts a baseline to cut variance. The plain gradient depends on the parametrisation. Write a Gaussian policy's spread as \(\sigma\) or as \(\log\sigma\) and the same data produce different updates. The natural gradient (Amari 1998; Kakade 2001) removes that dependence by measuring step size in the distance between policies rather than between parameters:

$$\Delta\theta \propto F^{-1}\nabla_\theta J, \qquad F = \mathbb E\big[\nabla\log\pi\,\nabla\log\pi^\top\big], \qquad \tfrac12\Delta\theta^\top F \Delta\theta \approx \mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta\,\Vert\,\pi_{\theta+\Delta\theta}).$$

It is the steepest ascent direction under a KL budget. TRPO enforces that budget explicitly, and PPO approximates it by clipping the probability ratio. Figure 3 runs both on a one-dimensional Gaussian policy with a bump reward. The two vanilla paths differ; the natural gradient traces the same path in both parametrisations, because its geometry belongs to the distributions, not the coordinates.

5. Learning rates that learn

Jacobs (1988) proposed delta-bar-delta: increase a parameter's step size while successive gradients agree in sign, and cut it when they flip. Herzfeld et al. (2014) found the same rule in people. In environments where a perturbation persisted from trial to trial, subjects became more sensitive to errors; where it switched often, they learned less from each error. They proposed that the nervous system keeps a memory of errors. Figure 4 runs delta-bar-delta on a perturbation that rarely switches and on one that often does. The learning rate settles at 0.14 in the first and 0.025 in the second, from the same start of 0.2.

For an adapting robot, the forgetting factor of an online estimator plays this role. It should be set by how fast the parameters drift, and that can be estimated from the sign structure of the errors (see Learning to Adapt).

6. When there is no gradient

Gains tuned on hardware, simulator parameters and anything behind a non-differentiable simulator are optimised from function values. The cross-entropy method and CMA-ES (Hansen's tutorial is the reference) adapt a sampling distribution's mean and covariance toward good samples. The covariance update is effectively a learned preconditioner, the zeroth-order cousin of the natural gradient. Bayesian optimisation fits a surrogate and spends samples where improvement is likely, which is how the one well-tuned fixed-gain baseline in my impedance review was built.

7. What to take away

  • Iterations scale with \(\kappa\) for gradient descent and \(\sqrt\kappa\) with momentum. Most "the optimiser is slow" problems are condition-number problems you created.
  • Adam undoes per-coordinate scaling, not coupling. Reparametrise coupled quantities.
  • Policy updates should be sized in KL, not parameter distance; that is what TRPO and PPO approximate.
  • Adapt step sizes to the consistency of the errors, as people do.
